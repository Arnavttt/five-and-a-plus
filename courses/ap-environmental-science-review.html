How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Enviro covers Earth systems, biodiversity, populations, land/water use, energy, pollution, and global change. The exam is very data-heavy roughly 40% of MCQs require interpreting graphs, tables, or maps. The FRQ section includes calculation problems (ENVI has more math than any other AP social science). You must know energy flow calculations, percent efficiency, and environmental data interpretation.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students make unit errors in calculation FRQs forgetting to convert kg to grams, or Watts to kilowatts-hours. Students also write vague causes for environmental problems ('pollution') instead of identifying the specific human activity and the specific pollutant. In the argue/justify FRQ, students take a position but fail to provide specific environmental evidence just restating the claim.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 student knows environmental vocabulary. A 5 student can apply ecological concepts quantitatively (ENPT equation for population growth, R factor and U-factor calculations, energy pyramid efficiency) AND explain the underlying mechanisms. Given data showing declining amphibian populations, a 5 student identifies multiple interacting causes (habitat fragmentation, chytrid fungus, UV exposure from ozone depletion) and ranks their relative importance.

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Master the calculation types: energy efficiency (trophic transfer, 10% rule), IPAT equation, percent change, unit conversion. Review all major environmental laws (Clean Air Act, Clean Water Act, ESA, CERCLA, CITES) and their mechanisms. Do the FRQ 'design a solution' question type know how to propose specific, measurable environmental interventions.

Exam-Day Skills

  • Calculation setup: Always show the equation, substitute values with units, and cancel units explicitly. APES graders award points for correct setup even if you make an arithmetic error.
  • Energy pyramid math: 10% rule means each trophic level has 10% of the energy of the one below it. If producers have 10,000 kcal, primary consumers have 1,000 kcal, secondary consumers have 100 kcal. Work backwards from a higher trophic level to calculate required producer biomass.
  • Data interpretation: Read graph axes and units carefully. Many APES MCQ errors come from misreading a y-axis as 'concentration' vs. 'rate of change' of concentration.
  • Identify-explain structure: For FRQ responses, follow this structure: 'One cause is [specific cause]. This contributes to [problem] because [mechanism].' Vague identification without mechanistic explanation earns 0 points.
  • Solution specificity: When proposing environmental solutions, name specific policies, technologies, or practices (cap-and-trade, riparian buffer zones, integrated pest management) rather than generic answers ('reduce pollution').

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (60%): 80 MCQs in 90 min. Section II (40%): 3 FRQs in 70 min (~23 min each).

FRQ types:
• Free Response with data/calculation (~10 pts): Includes at least one calculation question requiring correct setup, unit analysis, and numerical answer. Other parts require explaining environmental concepts, identifying causes and effects, and proposing solutions.
• Document/Scenario Analysis (~10 pts): Uses a graph, map, or environmental scenario. Requires data interpretation, concept application, and evaluation of policies or interventions.
• Argue/Justify (~10 pts): Take a position on an environmental policy or issue. Argue FOR and/or AGAINST with specific environmental evidence. Must go beyond opinion to cite specific data, mechanisms, or real-world examples.

Calculation scoring: Show your equation, substitute values with correct units, cancel units explicitly, and report the answer with correct units. Points awarded for correct setup even with arithmetic errors.

Explanation specificity: 'Explain' questions require mechanistic answers. 'Fertilizer runoff causes algal blooms' earns 1 pt; 'excess nitrogen from fertilizer runoff causes eutrophication: algal overgrowth depletes dissolved oxygen, creating hypoxic dead zones' earns full credit.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. The greenhouse effect refers to:

  • A. The destruction of the ozone layer by CFCs
  • B. The trapping of heat in Earth's atmosphere by gases that absorb and re-emit infrared radiation
  • C. The warming of greenhouses used in agriculture
  • D. The effect of solar radiation on ocean temperatures
Show Answer

Answer: B: Greenhouse gases (CO2, CH4, N2O, H2O vapor) absorb outgoing infrared radiation and re-emit it in all directions, warming Earth's surface a natural process amplified by human emissions.

Q2. Which of the following is the primary anthropogenic source of CO2 in the atmosphere?

  • A. Volcanic eruptions
  • B. Combustion of fossil fuels
  • C. Deforestation alone
  • D. Ocean outgassing
Show Answer

Answer: B: Burning coal, oil, and natural gas for energy releases CO2 that was sequestered for millions of years, accounting for ~75% of human-caused greenhouse gas emissions.

Q3. The concept of 'ecological footprint' measures:

  • A. The total area of land an animal's territory covers
  • B. The amount of biologically productive land and water required to sustain a person or population's consumption and absorb its waste
  • C. The impact of human activity on local ecosystems only
  • D. A country's total carbon emissions
Show Answer

Answer: B: Ecological footprint calculates the total productive land/sea area needed to produce all resources consumed and absorb all waste generated by an individual, city, or nation.

Q4. Primary productivity in an ecosystem refers to:

  • A. The total energy consumed by primary consumers
  • B. The rate at which producers (plants, algae) convert solar energy to biomass through photosynthesis
  • C. The number of trophic levels in a food chain
  • D. The rate of decomposition of organic matter
Show Answer

Answer: B: Net primary productivity (NPP) = gross photosynthesis - plant respiration. It represents the energy available to consumers in an ecosystem and varies widely by ecosystem type.

Q5. The 10% rule in ecological energy transfer means that:

  • A. 10% of all species go extinct each year
  • B. Only about 10% of energy is transferred from one trophic level to the next
  • C. 10% of sunlight reaches the ground
  • D. Decomposers process 10% of all dead matter
Show Answer

Answer: B: The 10% rule: on average, only ~10% of energy stored at one trophic level is incorporated into the next the rest is lost as heat (metabolism), to decomposers, or as indigestible matter. This limits the number of trophic levels.

Q6. Eutrophication of water bodies is primarily caused by:

  • A. Acid deposition from sulfur dioxide emissions
  • B. Excess nutrients (especially nitrogen and phosphorus) from agricultural runoff stimulating algal growth
  • C. Industrial heavy metal pollution
  • D. Climate change warming water temperatures
Show Answer

Answer: B: Eutrophication: excessive nutrient loading (fertilizer runoff, sewage) causes algal blooms, which deplete oxygen when they decompose, creating hypoxic (dead) zones that kill aquatic life.

Q7. The ozone layer (stratospheric ozone) is important because it:

  • A. Keeps Earth's temperature stable
  • B. Absorbs harmful ultraviolet (UV-B) radiation from the sun, protecting life on Earth
  • C. Reflects solar radiation back into space
  • D. Prevents acid rain from forming
Show Answer

Answer: B: Stratospheric ozone (O3) absorbs UV-B radiation (280-315 nm) that would otherwise damage DNA, cause skin cancer, and harm marine phytoplankton. CFCs catalytically destroy ozone molecules.

Q8. Biomagnification refers to:

  • A. The increase in population size of a species
  • B. The increasing concentration of persistent pollutants (e.g., DDT, mercury) in organisms at successively higher trophic levels
  • C. The magnification of greenhouse effects over time
  • D. The spread of invasive species through food webs
Show Answer

Answer: B: Biomagnification: fat-soluble persistent pollutants (DDT, PCBs, methylmercury) accumulate in organisms faster than they're excreted. As larger organisms eat many smaller ones, concentrations increase at each trophic level reaching highest levels in apex predators (including humans).

Q9. Which of the following is a renewable energy source?

  • A. Natural gas
  • B. Solar photovoltaic electricity
  • C. Coal
  • D. Uranium for nuclear power
Show Answer

Answer: B: Solar, wind, hydroelectric, and geothermal energy are renewable they are replenished naturally on human timescales. Fossil fuels and uranium are non-renewable (finite geological deposits).

Q10. The tragedy of the commons (Hardin) describes:

  • A. Environmental destruction by governments
  • B. The overexploitation of shared, open-access resources by self-interested individuals acting rationally
  • C. The failure of private enterprise to produce public goods
  • D. The collapse of fisheries due to pollution
Show Answer

Answer: B: Hardin's tragedy: in open-access commons (fisheries, atmosphere, groundwater), each individual benefits from using more while sharing costs of depletion among all rational self-interest leads to collective overexploitation.

Q11. Soil erosion is most effectively prevented by:

  • A. Conventional tillage farming
  • B. Cover cropping, contour plowing, terracing, and no-till agriculture
  • C. Increased irrigation
  • D. Removing vegetation to expose topsoil to sunlight
Show Answer

Answer: B: Sustainable soil management: cover crops hold soil between growing seasons; contour plowing slows runoff; terracing on slopes reduces erosion; no-till agriculture preserves soil structure and organic matter.

Q12. Which of the following best describes 'sustainable development'?

  • A. Economic growth without any environmental impact
  • B. Development that meets the needs of the present without compromising the ability of future generations to meet their own needs
  • C. Development exclusively in economically poor nations
  • D. Preservation of all natural areas without human use
Show Answer

Answer: B: The Brundtland Commission (1987) definition: sustainable development balances economic, social, and environmental needs across generations neither sacrificing development for environment nor environment for development.

Q13. The Clean Air Act (U.S.) primarily regulates:

  • A. Water pollution from industrial sources
  • B. Air pollutants including criteria pollutants (ozone, particulate matter, SO2, NOx, CO, lead) and toxic air pollutants
  • C. Carbon trading between states
  • D. Nuclear power plant emissions
Show Answer

Answer: B: The Clean Air Act (1970, amended 1990) establishes National Ambient Air Quality Standards (NAAQS) for six criteria pollutants, regulates emission sources, and addresses acid rain and ozone depletion.

Q14. IPAT equation (I = P × A × T) shows that environmental impact is a function of:

  • A. Income, Population, and Agriculture
  • B. Population size, Affluence (consumption per person), and Technology (impact per unit consumption)
  • C. Industry, Politics, and Trade
  • D. Income, Production, and Transport
Show Answer

Answer: B: IPAT: Environmental Impact = Population × Affluence (consumption) × Technology (environmental impact per unit consumption). Reducing any factor reduces impact; T can be negative if technology is sufficiently clean.

Q15. Which best describes 'biodiversity hotspots'?

  • A. Areas with the highest total biomass
  • B. Regions with exceptionally high species richness and endemism that are also under serious threat
  • C. Tropical forests with the highest rainfall
  • D. Areas with the greatest number of endangered species anywhere on Earth
Show Answer

Answer: B: Myers' biodiversity hotspots: 35+ regions with at least 1,500 endemic vascular plant species AND having lost at least 70% of primary habitat. They harbor ~60% of the world's plant, bird, mammal, reptile, and amphibian species on just 2.4% of Earth's land.

Q16. The nitrogen cycle's critical role in ecosystems is:

  • A. Providing oxygen for cellular respiration
  • B. Converting atmospheric N2 into biologically available forms (NH3, NO3-) that plants and microbes can use
  • C. Producing CO2 for photosynthesis
  • D. Regulating water pH in aquatic systems
Show Answer

Answer: B: Nitrogen fixation (by Rhizobium bacteria, cyanobacteria, and lightning) converts N2 gas to NH3 the entry point for nitrogen into the biological nitrogen cycle. Without it, nitrogen availability severely limits plant growth.

Q17. Ocean acidification is primarily caused by:

  • A. Thermal pollution from power plants
  • B. Absorption of anthropogenic CO2 by seawater, forming carbonic acid and reducing pH
  • C. Acid rain falling directly into oceans
  • D. Increased volcanic activity on the ocean floor
Show Answer

Answer: B: CO2 + H2O → H2CO3 → H+ + HCO3-. Dissolving CO2 increases [H+], reducing pH. Ocean pH has dropped ~0.1 units (30% more acidic) since pre-industrial times, threatening calcifying organisms (coral, shellfish, pteropods).

Q18. Which best explains why tropical rainforests have high biodiversity?

  • A. They have the richest soils on Earth
  • B. High, consistent solar energy input, high rainfall, and stable climate over millions of years allowed for extensive ecological specialization
  • C. They are isolated from other ecosystems
  • D. They have few predators, allowing species to diversify
Show Answer

Answer: B: Tropical stability over millennia allowed evolution of highly specialized niches, mutualistic relationships (pollinators, seed dispersers), and species packing. High productivity supports complex food webs; the consistent environment reduces extinction pressure from climatic fluctuations.

Q19. The Montreal Protocol (1987) successfully addressed:

  • A. Climate change by limiting CO2 emissions
  • B. The depletion of stratospheric ozone by phasing out CFC production and use
  • C. Ocean acidification
  • D. Loss of tropical biodiversity
Show Answer

Answer: B: The Montreal Protocol required signatory nations to phase out chlorofluorocarbons (CFCs) and other ozone-depleting substances. The ozone layer has shown measurable recovery one of environmental policy's great successes.

Q20. Which of the following is an example of a 'point source' water pollutant?

  • A. Agricultural runoff carrying pesticides over a wide area
  • B. Treated wastewater discharged from a pipe at a specific sewage treatment plant
  • C. Acid deposition from air pollution
  • D. Road surface runoff entering storm drains across a city
Show Answer

Answer: B: Point sources: pollution discharged from a single identifiable location (pipe, chimney). Nonpoint sources (agricultural runoff, urban stormwater) come from dispersed, hard-to-identify origins and are harder to regulate.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

a) Explain the process of eutrophication and its effects on aquatic ecosystems. b) Identify TWO human activities that contribute to eutrophication and explain how each contributes. c) Propose and explain TWO strategies to reduce eutrophication in a lake receiving agricultural runoff.
View Model Answer
a) Eutrophication is the process by which excess nutrients (primarily nitrogen and phosphorus) enter a water body, stimulating the rapid growth of algae and cyanobacteria (algal blooms). These blooms block sunlight from reaching submerged aquatic plants, killing them. When the massive algal biomass dies, bacteria decompose it through aerobic respiration, consuming oxygen from the water. This dramatically reduces dissolved oxygen concentrations (hypoxia or anoxia), creating 'dead zones' where most aquatic organisms cannot survive fish kills result. The process also promotes growth of cyanobacteria that produce toxins harmful to animals and humans, and shifts ecosystems from diverse, productive states to degraded, algae-dominated states with reduced biodiversity. b) Activity 1: Agricultural fertilizer application: Farmers apply nitrogen and phosphorus fertilizers to crops. Rain washes excess nutrients from fields into streams and lakes through surface runoff and leaching through soil into groundwater. Particularly after heavy rain events, large pulses of nutrients enter waterways. Concentrated animal feeding operations (CAFOs) generate manure that is land-applied, and the liquid portion (nutrient-rich) enters waterways. Activity 2: Sewage discharge and septic systems: Municipal wastewater treatment plants release effluent containing residual nitrogen and phosphorus into receiving waters. Inadequately maintained or improperly sited septic systems leach nutrients directly into groundwater that feeds lakes and streams. Combined sewer overflows during heavy rain events release untreated sewage directly to waterways. c) Strategy 1: Riparian buffer strips: Plant vegetated buffer zones (native grasses, shrubs, trees) along stream banks and lake edges adjacent to agricultural fields. These buffers physically filter surface runoff, absorbing nutrients before they reach the water body. Plant roots take up nitrogen and phosphorus; microbial communities in the soil convert nitrogen to N2 gas (denitrification). Studies show properly designed riparian buffers can reduce nitrogen runoff by 50-90%. Strategy 2: Precision fertilizer application: Farmers can reduce nutrient loading by testing soil before fertilizing and applying only the amount of fertilizer that crops actually need (variable rate application). Applying fertilizer in split doses throughout the growing season rather than all at once reduces the nutrient excess available for runoff. Timing applications to avoid periods of heavy rain and using slow-release fertilizer formulations further reduces leaching and runoff into the lake.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

a) Define the term 'carbon sequestration' and describe ONE natural carbon sink. b) Explain how deforestation contributes to climate change through TWO different mechanisms. c) Propose ONE market-based policy and ONE regulatory policy to reduce deforestation rates, and explain how each would work.
View Model Answer
a) Carbon sequestration is the process of capturing and storing atmospheric carbon dioxide (CO2), removing it from the atmosphere and reducing its contribution to the greenhouse effect. Natural carbon sinks actively absorb more CO2 than they release. One major natural carbon sink is the world's forests: trees absorb CO2 through photosynthesis and store carbon in wood, roots, and leaves (and in soil organic matter when leaves decompose). The world's forests store approximately 860 gigatons of carbon more than double the amount currently in the atmosphere making them critical regulators of atmospheric CO2 concentrations. b) Mechanism 1: Release of stored carbon: When trees are cut and burned or left to decompose, the carbon stored in their biomass is released as CO2 and CH4 (a more potent greenhouse gas than CO2). Tropical forest destruction releases approximately 4-8 gigatons of CO2 per year making deforestation responsible for roughly 10-15% of annual anthropogenic greenhouse gas emissions. The larger and older the trees, the more carbon is released when they are removed. Mechanism 2: Loss of carbon absorption capacity: Living forests continuously absorb CO2 from the atmosphere through photosynthesis, acting as an ongoing carbon sink. When forests are cleared, this absorption capacity is eliminated. Converted land uses (agriculture, pasture) absorb far less carbon than the original forest, so atmospheric CO2 concentrations rise both from the direct release of stored carbon and from the loss of ongoing absorption. c) Market-based policy: REDD+ (Reducing Emissions from Deforestation and Forest Degradation): This international mechanism pays developing countries for verified reductions in deforestation relative to a historical baseline. Wealthy countries or carbon markets purchase carbon credits from countries that protect their forests. This creates financial incentives for forest-dependent communities and governments to maintain forests rather than clearing them for agriculture, providing an income stream that competes with the economic return from conversion. The mechanism works by making the ecosystem services of living forests financially valuable. Regulatory policy: Mandatory environmental impact assessments (EIA) and deforestation licensing: Government regulations can require that any clearing of forested land above a threshold size undergo a mandatory environmental impact assessment before permits are issued. Brazil's Forest Code (requiring landowners in the Amazon to maintain 80% of their land in native vegetation) is an example. By legally prohibiting unauthorized clearing and establishing monitoring systems (such as satellite-based deforestation detection like Brazil's PRODES system), governments can directly limit deforestation. Enforcement through fines, equipment seizures, and criminal prosecution for violations makes compliance the economically rational choice for landowners.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

a) Explain the difference between nonrenewable and renewable energy sources, giving ONE example of each. b) Explain TWO environmental advantages of solar photovoltaic energy over coal-fired electricity generation. c) Explain ONE economic barrier to rapid adoption of renewable energy and suggest ONE policy that could address it.
View Model Answer
a) Nonrenewable energy sources are formed over geological timescales (millions of years) and are consumed far faster than they can be replenished once used, they are effectively gone. Example: Coal is a fossil fuel formed from compressed ancient plant matter over 300+ million years; at current consumption rates, reserves will be exhausted within centuries. Renewable energy sources are replenished naturally on human timescales sunlight, wind, water flow, and geothermal heat are continuously available. Example: Solar photovoltaic (PV) energy converts sunlight directly to electricity using semiconductor cells; sunlight is inexhaustible on any relevant human timescale. b) Environmental advantage 1: Dramatically lower greenhouse gas emissions: Coal-fired power plants emit approximately 820 grams of CO2 per kilowatt-hour of electricity generated (plus additional emissions from coal mining). Solar PV emits approximately 20-50 g CO2-equivalent per kWh across its full life cycle (manufacturing, installation, operation, disposal). This 15-40x reduction in lifecycle emissions makes solar critical for decarbonizing electricity generation and mitigating climate change. Environmental advantage 2: No air pollution during operation: Coal combustion releases sulfur dioxide (SO2), nitrogen oxides (NOx), particulate matter, mercury, and other toxic pollutants that cause acid rain, respiratory disease, and ecosystem damage. Solar PV generates electricity with no operational air emissions no sulfur, no particulates, no mercury. This produces substantial public health co-benefits: reduced respiratory illness, cardiovascular disease, and premature mortality in populations near power plants. c) Economic barrier: High upfront capital costs: Solar installations require significant upfront investment for panels, inverters, installation, and grid connection, even though operating costs are near-zero. Many households and businesses lack access to capital for this investment, and the payback period (typically 6-12 years) may deter investment even when lifetime costs are lower than grid electricity. Developing countries face additional barriers from limited capital markets. Policy solution: Low-interest green financing programs and on-bill financing: Government-backed green banks or programs like PACE (Property Assessed Clean Energy) financing allow property owners to borrow money for solar installations and repay through their property tax bill or utility bill, with no upfront cost. Loan repayment comes from energy savings, making the investment cash-flow positive from day one. This addresses the capital access barrier without requiring grants or subsidies, using the guaranteed energy savings as collateral for the loan.

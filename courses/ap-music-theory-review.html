How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Music Theory tests written and aural skills equally (each 45% of score). Written skills: notating rhythms and pitches, identifying and writing intervals, chords (triads and 7th chords), scales, key signatures, figured bass realization, harmonic analysis using Roman numerals, part-writing (four-part SATB chorale style), melody harmonization, and melody composition. Aural skills: sight-singing (solfège or numbers), melodic dictation, rhythmic dictation, and harmonic dictation.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students lose points in part-writing by committing parallel fifths or parallel octaves between any two voices. Voice leading errors (augmented second in minor, large leaps without resolution, doubled leading tone) each cost points. In sight-singing, students rush through difficult intervals instead of subdividing. In harmonic analysis, students mis-analyze inversions because they don't correctly identify bass note vs. chord root.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 student knows music theory rules. A 5 student has internalized voice leading so thoroughly that errors sound wrong before being analyzed. A 5 sight-singer keeps steady tempo even through difficult passages and applies solfège consistently rather than guessing. The aural skills section separates average from excellent scores daily ear training practice is the only path to consistent 5s.

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Sight-sing for 20 minutes every day use a metronome. Practice four-part harmonization of chorale-style melodies, checking every vertical interval and every resolution. Review deceptive cadences (V→vi), half cadences (ending on V), authentic cadences (V→I). Practice writing suspensions (4-3, 7-6, 2-3 retardation). Do 3 complete written FRQ sets under timed conditions.

Exam-Day Skills

  • Part-writing checklist: After writing each chord, check: (1) No parallel 5ths or 8ths between any pair of voices. (2) No direct 5ths or 8ths in outer voices. (3) No augmented 2nd in any voice (especially in minor). (4) Leading tone resolves up by step in V→I. (5) 7th of chord resolves down by step.
  • Sight-singing strategy: Establish the tonal center by singing the tonic chord before beginning. Mark scale degrees in your head. Subdivide difficult rhythms tap or conduct while singing. Don't stop if you make an error continue in time.
  • Harmonic dictation: Listen for bass line first (most distinctive), then identify chord quality (major/minor/diminished), then inversion. Write Roman numerals with figured bass numbers from the bass note up.
  • Figured bass realization: Know what each figure means above the bass: 6 = first inversion triad; 6/4 = second inversion triad; 7 = root position 7th chord; 6/5, 4/3, 4/2 = inversions of 7th chord. Fill upper voices avoiding parallels.
  • Melodic dictation: Listen for the cadence first (phrase length). Then listen for the shape of the opening gesture. On second hearing, fill in the gaps. Check intervals against the scale most melodies are diatonic with few chromatic notes.

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (45%): Multiple-choice and free-response written theory questions (notation, analysis, part-writing). Section II (45%): Aural skills: sight-singing (performed in front of examiner), melodic dictation, rhythmic dictation, and harmonic dictation.

Written Theory scoring:
• Part-writing (~18 pts): Each voice-leading error (parallel 5ths/8ths, doubled leading tone, unresolved 7th, augmented 2nd) loses points. Correct chord progression, correct doubling, and smooth voice leading each earn pts.
• Harmonic analysis (~12 pts): Correct Roman numeral (including chord quality and inversion) = 1 pt per chord. Partially correct (right root, wrong inversion) = partial credit.
• Melody completion/harmonization (~10 pts): Scored on: melodic smoothness, appropriate harmony, correct voice leading, and use of standard cadence patterns at phrase endings.

Aural Skills scoring:
• Sight-singing (~15 pts): Scored holistically on pitch accuracy, rhythmic accuracy, and tempo steadiness. Points lost for each incorrect pitch or rhythmic value.
• Melodic/rhythmic dictation (~12 pts): Each correct note = 1 pt; pitch errors and rhythm errors each lose points independently.
• Harmonic dictation (~8 pts): Each correct Roman numeral (including inversion) = 1 pt.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. A major scale is constructed with the interval pattern:

  • A. W-W-H-W-W-W-H
  • B. W-H-W-W-H-W-W
  • C. W-W-W-H-W-W-H
  • D. H-W-W-W-H-W-W
Show Answer

Answer: A: Major scale: Whole-Whole-Half-Whole-Whole-Whole-Half (W-W-H-W-W-W-H). From C: C-D-E-F-G-A-B-C. The pattern creates the characteristic major (happy) sound.

Q2. In standard notation, a dotted quarter note has a duration equal to:

  • A. 1.5 beats
  • B. 3 beats
  • C. 2 beats
  • D. A quarter plus an eighth note
Show Answer

Answer: A: A dot increases a note's value by half. A quarter note = 1 beat; half of 1 beat = 0.5 beat; dotted quarter = 1.5 beats. Equivalently, a dotted quarter = a quarter plus an eighth note.

Q3. The interval from C to G is a:

  • A. Perfect fourth
  • B. Perfect fifth
  • C. Major sixth
  • D. Minor seventh
Show Answer

Answer: B: C to G spans 7 half-steps (C-C#-D-D#-E-F-F#-G) or counts as the 5th scale degree of the C major scale. Intervals with 'perfect' quality (unison, 4th, 5th, octave) don't have major/minor versions they are perfect, augmented, or diminished.

Q4. Counterpoint refers to:

  • A. The study of chord progressions
  • B. The technique of combining two or more melodic lines that are rhythmically and harmonically independent yet interdependent
  • C. The use of syncopation in rhythm
  • D. Playing louder than the written dynamic marking
Show Answer

Answer: B: Counterpoint (polyphony): the art of combining independent melodic lines simultaneously. Bach's fugues are the pinnacle of Western counterpoint; Palestrina's vocal polyphony and Baroque inventions all demonstrate contrapuntal technique.

Q5. A V7 chord in C major consists of:

  • A. G-B-D
  • B. G-B-D-F
  • C. G-B-D-F#
  • D. G-A-B-D
Show Answer

Answer: B: The dominant seventh chord V7 in C major is built on the 5th scale degree (G) with a minor 7th added: G-B-D-F. The tritone between B and F creates strong tension that resolves to the tonic (C-E-G).

Q6. Sonata form is structured as:

  • A. Theme, development, recapitulation
  • B. Exposition, development, recapitulation
  • C. Introduction, verse, chorus, bridge
  • D. A-B-A form with a contrasting middle section
Show Answer

Answer: B: Sonata form (Classical period): Exposition (presents 2 contrasting themes in tonic and dominant), Development (themes fragmented, modulated, developed), Recapitulation (both themes return in the tonic), often with a Coda. Foundation of Classical symphonies, sonatas, and string quartets.

Q7. The relative minor key of C major is:

  • A. C minor
  • B. A minor
  • C. G minor
  • D. E minor
Show Answer

Answer: B: Every major key has a relative minor that shares its key signature. The relative minor starts on the 6th scale degree of the major scale. The 6th degree of C major = A. A natural minor: A-B-C-D-E-F-G-A using the same notes as C major with no sharps or flats.

Q8. Chromaticism in music refers to:

  • A. Music written only in the key of C
  • B. The use of pitches outside the prevailing diatonic key, including all 12 half-steps
  • C. Music that uses only the pentatonic scale
  • D. The use of very fast melodic passages
Show Answer

Answer: B: Chromaticism: using notes foreign to the prevailing key (chromatic = all 12 half-steps). Chromatic passing tones, borrowed chords, and chromatic harmony (Wagner, late Romantic) increase harmonic tension and expressivity.

Q9. The term 'homophony' describes musical texture in which:

  • A. All voices are melodically independent
  • B. One melody is supported by chordal accompaniment (melody + harmony)
  • C. Multiple voices sing the same melody in unison
  • D. The texture is very thin with few instruments
Show Answer

Answer: B: Homophony: a primary melody supported by chords or a subordinate accompaniment the most common texture in popular music and many Classical works. Contrasts with polyphony (multiple equal melodic lines) and monophony (single melody, no accompaniment).

Q10. A 'cadence' in music is:

  • A. A passage of rapid scales
  • B. A harmonic progression creating a point of rest or arrival
  • C. A sudden change in tempo
  • D. A type of ornamentation
Show Answer

Answer: B: Cadence: a harmonic formula marking phrase endings or major structural points. Perfect authentic cadence (V-I): strongest closure. Half cadence (ends on V): temporary rest. Deceptive cadence (V-vi): surprises the ear.

Q11. The circle of fifths organizes keys by:

  • A. Alphabetical order
  • B. Moving up by perfect fifths (clockwise) or perfect fourths (counterclockwise), with adjacent keys sharing all but one pitch
  • C. Distance from middle C
  • D. Number of accidentals only
Show Answer

Answer: B: Circle of fifths: C major (no sharps/flats) → G major (1 sharp, F#) → D major (2 sharps) → etc. Each clockwise step adds a sharp; counterclockwise adds a flat. Adjacent keys are closely related; opposite keys (tritone apart) are most distantly related.

Q12. The texture of Bach's two-part inventions is best described as:

  • A. Homophonic
  • B. Polyphonic (contrapuntal) two independent and equal melodic voices
  • C. Monophonic
  • D. Heterophonic
Show Answer

Answer: B: Bach's Two-Part Inventions are contrapuntal (polyphonic): each hand plays an independent but related melodic line. The voices imitate, respond to, and complement each other while maintaining melodic independence.

Q13. The term 'dynamics' in music refers to:

  • A. Tempo changes
  • B. Variations in loudness (ppp to fff) and the directives that control them (crescendo, diminuendo)
  • C. The emotional mood of a piece
  • D. Changes in key signature
Show Answer

Answer: B: Dynamics: the loudness of music. Terms from softest to loudest: ppp (pianississimo), pp (pianissimo), p (piano), mp (mezzo-piano), mf (mezzo-forte), f (forte), ff (fortissimo), fff (fortississimo). Crescendo = gradually louder; decrescendo/diminuendo = gradually softer.

Q14. Which time signature indicates 6 eighth-note beats organized into two groups of 3?

  • A. 6/8
  • B. 3/4
  • C. 2/4
  • D. 6/4
Show Answer

Answer: A: 6/8: 6 beats per measure with the eighth note as the beat unit. At faster tempos, these are felt as 2 beats (dotted quarter notes), each subdivided into 3 eighth notes compound duple time. This gives 6/8 its characteristic lilting, rocking feel.

Q15. A 'mode' in music theory refers to:

  • A. The key signature alone
  • B. A scale pattern beginning on a specific degree of the major scale, producing a characteristic set of intervallic relationships
  • C. The tempo of a piece
  • D. The form of a composition
Show Answer

Answer: B: Church modes: Dorian (D to D on white keys), Phrygian (E to E), Lydian (F to F), Mixolydian (G to G), Aeolian = natural minor (A to A), Locrian (B to B). Each has a unique pattern of whole and half steps giving it a distinctive character.

Q16. The leading tone in a major scale is:

  • A. The 5th scale degree
  • B. The 7th scale degree, a half step below the tonic
  • C. The 6th scale degree
  • D. The 3rd scale degree
Show Answer

Answer: B: The leading tone (7th scale degree) is a half step below the tonic and has a strong melodic tendency to resolve upward to the tonic by step. In C major: B is the leading tone, resolving to C. This resolution is central to tonal harmony.

Q17. Enharmonic equivalents are pitches that:

  • A. Sound the same but are spelled differently (e.g., C# and D♭)
  • B. Are an octave apart
  • C. Have the same name but different pitch
  • D. Sound different but are written the same
Show Answer

Answer: A: Enharmonic equivalents: same pitch, different spelling. C# = D♭; F# = G♭; G# = A♭. In equal temperament, enharmonic spellings are acoustically identical but functionally different in tonal harmony the spelling indicates the pitch's function and its expected resolution.

Q18. The term 'ritardando' (rit.) indicates:

  • A. Getting gradually louder
  • B. Getting gradually slower
  • C. A sudden accent
  • D. A return to the original tempo
Show Answer

Answer: B: Ritardando (rit.) = gradually slowing down. Related: rallentando (rall.) = gradually slowing, slightly more extreme; accelerando (accel.) = gradually speeding up; a tempo = return to original tempo after a change.

Q19. In Western tonal harmony, the subdominant chord is built on:

  • A. The 1st scale degree
  • B. The 4th scale degree
  • C. The 5th scale degree
  • D. The 2nd scale degree
Show Answer

Answer: B: Subdominant (IV): built on the 4th scale degree. In C major: F-A-C. The IV chord has a 'pre-dominant' function, naturally leading toward the V chord, which then resolves to I. The I-IV-V-I progression is fundamental to tonal harmony and blues/rock music.

Q20. A fugue is characterized by:

  • A. A theme played by all voices simultaneously from the start
  • B. A subject presented by one voice, then imitated in other voices at different pitch levels, with development through episodes
  • C. A simple A-B-A form
  • D. A single voice accompanied by chords
Show Answer

Answer: B: Fugue: a contrapuntal form where a subject (theme) is introduced by one voice, then answered by successive voices at the dominant (or tonic), with episodes (passages without the subject) connecting subject entries. Bach's Well-Tempered Clavier contains 48 fugues.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

(a) Write out the G major scale ascending and descending in treble clef notation (describe the notes and accidentals). (b) Identify the key signature for G major and explain how to determine key signatures using the circle of fifths. (c) Build a I, IV, and V chord in G major. Name the notes in each chord and identify the quality (major or minor).
View Model Answer
(a) G major scale: G - A - B - C - D - E - F# - G (ascending), then G - F# - E - D - C - B - A - G (descending). The pattern of whole and half steps: G-A (W), A-B (W), B-C (H), C-D (W), D-E (W), E-F# (W), F#-G (H). The only accidental is F#, which is required to maintain the W-W-H-W-W-W-H pattern. (b) G major key signature: 1 sharp (F#), placed on the top line of the treble staff. Using the circle of fifths: starting from C major (no sharps or flats), moving clockwise by perfect fifths C → G. G major has 1 sharp. D major (next clockwise) has 2 sharps (F#, C#). Each clockwise step adds one sharp; the order of sharps is F-C-G-D-A-E-B (Father Charles Goes Down And Ends Battle). To read sharp key signatures: the last sharp is the leading tone; the tonic is one half-step above it. One sharp = F#; one above F# = G major. c) Chords in G major: I chord (tonic): G - B - D. Quality: major (built on scale degree 1; the major third G-B and perfect fifth G-D make it major). IV chord (subdominant): C - E - G. Quality: major (built on scale degree 4; C-E is a major third, C-G is a perfect fifth). V chord (dominant): D - F# - A. Quality: major (built on scale degree 5; D-F# is a major third, D-A is a perfect fifth). Note: V7 adds C (the minor 7th): D-F#-A-C, creating strong tension that resolves to I.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

Listen to (or imagine) the following harmonic progression in C major: C major - A minor - F major - G major (I - vi - IV - V). (a) Describe the function of each chord in tonal harmony (tonic, subdominant, dominant function). (b) Explain why the V chord creates tension and resolves naturally to I. (c) Identify TWO popular songs or classical works that use this progression and explain its appeal.
View Model Answer
(a) Chord functions in I-vi-IV-V (C-Am-F-G in C major): I (C major): tonic function home base, stability, rest. The progression begins and typically ends here. vi (A minor): tonic function the relative minor is closely related to I (shares two notes: C-E-G and A-C-E), providing variety without departing far from stability. iv (F major): subdominant function pre-dominant, creates a sense of motion away from home, building toward the dominant. V (G major): dominant function creates the strongest tension and harmonic expectation of resolution back to I. The V chord's leading tone (B, the 7th scale degree) is a half-step below the tonic C and has a strong melodic pull upward; the 4th of the chord (F, if V7 = G-B-D-F) pulls downward to E of I. (b) The V chord (G-B-D in C major) creates tension through the tritone interval between the leading tone (B) and the 4th above (F in V7 = G-B-D-F). A tritone (augmented 4th/diminished 5th) is the most dissonant interval in tonal harmony it demands resolution. In V7→I: B resolves up to C (leading tone → tonic); F resolves down to E (4th → 3rd of I). This contrary motion of the tritone resolving inward to a major 3rd (C-E) creates the powerful sense of arrival and rest on the tonic. This is the fundamental engine of tonal harmony. c) The I-vi-IV-V progression (called the '50s progression' or 'doo-wop changes') is ubiquitous. In classical music: early period Haydn and Mozart frequently employ this harmonic framework. In popular music: The Beatles' 'Please Please Me' and countless others. The progression's appeal is its mixture of stability (tonic I and its relative vi) with motion (subdominant IV) and tension-resolution (dominant V-I), creating a satisfying, complete harmonic journey. Its simplicity makes it instantly singable; its harmonic logic makes it universally satisfying across centuries and genres.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

(a) Describe the main characteristics that distinguish Baroque (1600-1750) music from Classical (1750-1820) music. (b) Identify ONE specific Baroque composer and ONE specific Classical composer and give an example work by each that illustrates the characteristics of their period. (c) Explain how the development of the piano affected Classical period compositional style.
View Model Answer
(a) Baroque characteristics: Basso continuo (improvised harmonic accompaniment from bass line); terraced dynamics (sudden shifts between loud and soft rather than gradual changes); ornamentation (trills, mordents, turns added to melodic lines); polyphonic texture (multiple equal melodic lines, especially fugue and counterpoint); modal/tonal harmonic language still in development; characteristic genres: suite, concerto grosso, cantata, oratorio, fugue, toccata. Classical characteristics: Homophonic texture (melody + chordal accompaniment) generally prevails; gradual dynamics (crescendo and diminuendo through the orchestra); lighter, more transparent texture; periodic phrase structure (balanced 4- and 8-measure phrases); clear formal designs (sonata form, theme and variations, rondo); characteristic genres: symphony, string quartet, piano sonata, classical concerto. The shift reflects Enlightenment aesthetic values: clarity, balance, rational organization, and accessibility over complexity. (b) Baroque: Johann Sebastian Bach (1685-1750). The Well-Tempered Clavier, Book I (1722) exemplifies Baroque polyphony: 24 paired preludes and fugues in all 24 major and minor keys, demonstrating equal temperament tuning and contrapuntal mastery. Each fugue presents a subject imitated in multiple voices, with episodes and development the pinnacle of Western counterpoint. Classical: Wolfgang Amadeus Mozart (1756-1791). Symphony No. 40 in G minor, K. 550 (1788) exemplifies Classical style: four-movement structure, clear sonata form in the first movement with two contrasting themes, balanced phrase structure, orchestral transparency allowing individual instrumental voices to emerge, and dramatic use of the minor mode for emotional intensity within formal Classical constraints. c) The fortepiano (early piano, perfected by Cristofori c. 1700) replaced the harpsichord as the primary keyboard instrument during the Classical period, fundamentally changing compositional possibilities. Unlike the harpsichord (which produced a constant volume regardless of touch), the piano's hammer mechanism allowed dynamic variation responsive to key pressure hence 'pianoforte' (soft-loud). This enabled: gradual dynamics (crescendo/diminuendo) written for the keyboard; singing cantabile melodies over accompaniment; expressive accents and sforzando markings; a new type of keyboard texture with the Alberti bass (broken chord accompaniment pattern) supporting long lyrical melodies impossible to sustain expressively on harpsichord. Haydn's and Mozart's piano sonatas exploit the piano's dynamic range extensively; Beethoven's later sonatas pushed the piano's capabilities to their limits, anticipating Romantic style.

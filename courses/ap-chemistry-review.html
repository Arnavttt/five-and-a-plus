How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Chem tests atomic structure, periodicity, bonding/IMFs, stoichiometry, thermodynamics (ΔG, ΔH, ΔS), kinetics, equilibrium (ICE tables, Le Chatelier), acids/bases (pH, Ka/Kb, buffers), electrochemistry, and chemical reactions. The FRQ section is 50% of your score and requires multi-step quantitative reasoning and experimental justification. The exam provides a formula sheet and periodic table you must know WHEN to use each formula.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students set up ICE tables incorrectly forgetting to subtract x from reactants and add x to products, or using molar concentrations instead of initial molarity. Students lose significant figures points by rounding intermediate calculations. For electrochemistry, students reverse the sign of reduction potentials when calculating cell voltage for oxidation half-reactions. Students also write incorrect net ionic equations by failing to dissociate strong acids/bases and soluble salts.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 student knows formulas. A 5 student can diagnose which formula to use from context, set up multi-step calculations correctly, justify experimental observations with chemical reasoning, and explain unexpected data. In the FRQ 'justify your answer' sections, a 5 student always provides the mechanistic reasoning not just the conclusion.

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Run through one complete ICE table problem per day. Practice pH calculations for weak acids, weak bases, buffers (Henderson-Hasselbalch), and hydrolysis reactions. Review electrochemical cell notation and Nernst equation. Do full FRQ sets timed at 23 minutes per long FRQ. Know all polyatomic ions, solubility rules, and acid/base strength trends.

Exam-Day Skills

  • ICE table mastery: Always convert volume to liters and mass to moles before setting up ICE. Use x for small changes when Ka << initial concentration (5% approximation rule). Check your assumption: x/[initial] must be < 0.05.
  • Significant figures: In addition/subtraction, round to fewest decimal places. In multiplication/division, round to fewest significant figures. Never round until the final answer.
  • Electrochemistry sign convention: E°cell = E°cathode − E°anode (both from reduction potential tables). Positive E°cell means spontaneous. ΔG° = −nFE°cell. If E° is negative, the cell is non-spontaneous as written.
  • Thermodynamics: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS. Know all four sign combinations and spontaneity conditions. At equilibrium, ΔG = 0, so T = ΔH/ΔS for phase transitions.
  • Explain questions: Use chemical reasoning, not just conclusions. 'The reaction is spontaneous' earns no points. 'ΔG is negative because ΔH is negative and TΔS is positive, so both terms favor product formation' earns points.

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (50%): 60 MCQs in 90 min (includes multi-select MCQs worth 1 pt each). Section II (50%): 7 FRQs in 105 min (3 long + 4 short).

Long FRQ (~10 pts): Multi-part problems covering calculations, explanations, and experimental justification. Often requires: stoichiometric calculations, equilibrium ICE tables, thermodynamic predictions, electrochemical analysis, or kinetics work.

Short FRQ (~4 pts each): Usually 2 to 3 parts, one calculation and one explanation. May involve acid-base chemistry, IMFs, periodic trends, or spectroscopy.

Calculation scoring: Partial credit is awarded for correct setup and correct intermediate steps even if the final answer is wrong. Show ALL work. Include units at every step. Report final answers with correct significant figures.

'Explain' and 'justify' questions: These require chemical reasoning, not just conclusions. Name the specific chemical principle, apply it to the scenario, and state the predicted outcome. One-word or one-phrase answers almost never earn full credit.

Formula sheet: The AP Chemistry exam provides a formula sheet. Practice using it efficiently so you know exactly where to find each equation during the exam.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. The atomic number of an element determines:

  • A. Its mass number
  • B. The number of protons in the nucleus, defining the element's identity
  • C. Its number of neutrons
  • D. Its position in the periodic table only
Show Answer

Answer: B: The atomic number (Z) = number of protons. Since protons define element identity, Z uniquely identifies each element. Isotopes have same Z but different neutron numbers.

Q2. Which type of bond involves the sharing of electrons between nonmetal atoms?

  • A. Ionic bond
  • B. Covalent bond
  • C. Metallic bond
  • D. Hydrogen bond
Show Answer

Answer: B: Covalent bonds form when atoms share electron pairs. Nonmetals with similar electronegativities form covalent bonds rather than transferring electrons (which forms ionic bonds).

Q3. According to Le Chatelier's principle, increasing pressure on a gaseous equilibrium system will shift the reaction toward:

  • A. The side with more moles of gas
  • B. The side with fewer moles of gas
  • C. Increasing temperature
  • D. No effect pressure doesn't affect equilibrium
Show Answer

Answer: B: Le Chatelier's principle: increasing pressure on a gas-phase equilibrium shifts the reaction toward the side with fewer moles of gas to reduce the pressure (reduce the number of gas molecules).

Q4. Which quantum number describes the orientation of an orbital in space?

  • A. Principal quantum number (n)
  • B. Magnetic quantum number (ml)
  • C. Angular momentum quantum number (l)
  • D. Spin quantum number (ms)
Show Answer

Answer: B: The magnetic quantum number (ml) specifies the spatial orientation of an orbital. For a p subshell (l=1), ml = -1, 0, +1, corresponding to px, py, and pz orbitals.

Q5. The standard enthalpy of formation (ΔH°f) of an element in its standard state is:

  • A. Equal to zero by definition
  • B. Always positive
  • C. Equal to its heat of combustion
  • D. Equal to its ionization energy
Show Answer

Answer: A: By convention, the standard enthalpy of formation of any element in its most stable form at 25°C and 1 atm is defined as exactly zero. This provides the reference point for calculating reaction enthalpies.

Q6. A solution is buffered when it contains:

  • A. Only a strong acid and strong base
  • B. A weak acid and its conjugate base (or a weak base and its conjugate acid) in significant amounts
  • C. Only water and dissolved ions
  • D. A strong acid at low concentration
Show Answer

Answer: B: A buffer resists pH changes by containing both a weak acid (to neutralize added base) and its conjugate base (to neutralize added acid) in comparable amounts.

Q7. Which of the following has the highest first ionization energy?

  • A. Sodium (Na)
  • B. Fluorine (F)
  • C. Cesium (Cs)
  • D. Potassium (K)
Show Answer

Answer: B: Ionization energy increases across a period (more protons, stronger nuclear attraction) and decreases down a group (electrons farther from nucleus, more shielding). Fluorine is in period 2 and rightmost among nonmetals, giving it very high IE.

Q8. Entropy (S) increases in which of the following processes?

  • A. Freezing water
  • B. Combustion of a hydrocarbon producing gases
  • C. Dissolving sugar in water (always)
  • D. Formation of a crystal from solution
Show Answer

Answer: B: Combustion produces CO2 and H2O gas from solid/liquid reactants massive increase in disorder (entropy). More gas molecules with more possible arrangements = higher entropy.

Q9. The rate-determining step of a reaction mechanism is:

  • A. Always the last step
  • B. The slowest step, which determines the overall rate of the reaction
  • C. The step with the highest activation energy threshold that is always first
  • D. The step involving the most reactant molecules
Show Answer

Answer: B: In a multistep mechanism, the slowest step is the bottleneck its rate law determines the overall reaction rate. Intermediates formed in fast steps before the slow step don't appear in the rate law.

Q10. Which of the following is true of a galvanic (voltaic) cell?

  • A. Reduction occurs at the anode
  • B. Oxidation occurs at the anode, reduction at the cathode, producing spontaneous current
  • C. Electrons flow from cathode to anode
  • D. The cell reaction is non-spontaneous
Show Answer

Answer: B: In galvanic cells (batteries): oxidation occurs at the anode (negative terminal in galvanic); reduction at the cathode (positive terminal). Electrons flow anode→cathode through the external circuit. The reaction is spontaneous (ΔG < 0).

Q11. Hess's Law states that:

  • A. Heat of reaction depends on the pathway between reactants and products
  • B. The total enthalpy change for a reaction is the same regardless of the pathway (because enthalpy is a state function)
  • C. Exothermic reactions always proceed faster than endothermic ones
  • D. Entropy increases in all spontaneous reactions
Show Answer

Answer: B: Hess's Law: because enthalpy is a state function (depends only on initial and final states), ΔH for a reaction is the same whether it occurs in one step or multiple steps allowing calculation of hard-to-measure ΔH values.

Q12. The Ksp (solubility product constant) of a sparingly soluble salt AB is 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰. The molar solubility of AB in pure water is:

  • A. 1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰ M
  • B. 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M
  • C. 1.0 × 10⁻²⁰ M
  • D. 1.0 × 10⁻⁸ M
Show Answer

Answer: B: For AB → A+ + B-: Ksp = [A+][B-] = s × s = s². Therefore s = √(1.0 × 10⁻¹⁰) = 1.0 × 10⁻⁵ M.

Q13. VSEPR theory predicts the geometry of a molecule based on:

  • A. Bond lengths between atoms
  • B. Electron pair repulsion both bonding and lone pairs arrange to minimize repulsion
  • C. Electronegativity differences between bonded atoms
  • D. The hybridization of the central atom alone
Show Answer

Answer: B: VSEPR (Valence Shell Electron Pair Repulsion): electron pairs (bonding and lone pairs) arrange around the central atom to maximize distance between them, minimizing repulsion and determining molecular geometry.

Q14. A reaction with a negative ΔG° (standard Gibbs free energy) is:

  • A. Endothermic
  • B. Spontaneous under standard conditions
  • C. At equilibrium
  • D. Kinetically fast
Show Answer

Answer: B: ΔG = ΔH - TΔS. When ΔG < 0, the reaction releases free energy and is spontaneous (thermodynamically favorable) under standard conditions. Note: spontaneous ≠ fast kinetic barriers may still make the reaction slow.

Q15. Which intermolecular force is responsible for the unusually high boiling point of water (100°C) compared to similar molecules?

  • A. London dispersion forces
  • B. Hydrogen bonding between water molecules
  • C. Dipole-dipole interactions
  • D. Ionic bonding
Show Answer

Answer: B: Water's high boiling point, surface tension, and specific heat result from extensive hydrogen bonding (H-F, H-O, H-N). Each water molecule can form up to 4 H-bonds, requiring much more energy to vaporize than comparable molecules.

Q16. In a first-order reaction, the half-life (t1/2) is:

  • A. Dependent on the initial concentration
  • B. Constant and equal to ln(2)/k, independent of initial concentration
  • C. Equal to 1/k
  • D. Proportional to the square of the initial concentration
Show Answer

Answer: B: First-order half-life t1/2 = 0.693/k. This is constant and independent of concentration a defining characteristic of first-order kinetics. This applies to radioactive decay and many chemical and biological processes.

Q17. The oxidation state of chromium in K2Cr2O7 (potassium dichromate) is:

  • A. +2
  • B. +6
  • C. +3
  • D. +7
Show Answer

Answer: B: K2Cr2O7: K is +1 (×2 = +2), O is -2 (×7 = -14). Total must = 0: +2 + 2(Cr) + (-14) = 0 → 2(Cr) = +12 → Cr = +6.

Q18. Which of the following best explains why noble gases have very low reactivity?

  • A. They have very high electronegativity
  • B. They have complete valence electron shells (octet), with no tendency to gain, lose, or share electrons
  • C. They are gases at room temperature
  • D. They have very high ionization energies only
Show Answer

Answer: B: Noble gases have full valence shells (He: 2 electrons; Ne, Ar, etc.: 8 electrons) the stable electron configuration all other elements 'seek' through bonding. No energetically favorable bonding options exist for most noble gases.

Q19. The common ion effect on solubility refers to:

  • A. Adding a catalyst to increase solubility
  • B. Decreased solubility of a salt when a common ion is added to the solution
  • C. Increased solubility at higher temperatures
  • D. The effect of pH on acid-base equilibria
Show Answer

Answer: B: The common ion effect: adding a common ion (e.g., adding NaCl to a saturated AgCl solution adds Cl-) shifts the equilibrium toward the solid, decreasing solubility of the salt. This is a direct application of Le Chatelier's principle.

Q20. Acid strength of binary acids (H-X) increases as:

  • A. The H-X bond becomes stronger
  • B. The H-X bond becomes weaker and more polar (lower bond energy, making H+ easier to release)
  • C. Electronegativity of X decreases
  • D. The size of X decreases
Show Answer

Answer: B: Binary acid strength correlates with H-X bond strength: weaker bonds more easily release H+ (higher Ka). Down a group (HF < HCl < HBr < HI), bond energy decreases as size of X increases, making larger halides better acid components despite lower electronegativity.

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

A reaction between A(g) and B(g) was studied. Rate data: Experiment 1: [A]=0.10M, [B]=0.10M, Rate=2.0×10⁻³ M/s Experiment 2: [A]=0.20M, [B]=0.10M, Rate=4.0×10⁻³ M/s Experiment 3: [A]=0.10M, [B]=0.20M, Rate=8.0×10⁻³ M/s a) Determine the rate law, including the order with respect to each reactant. b) Calculate the rate constant k and include units. c) Predict the rate when [A]=0.30M and [B]=0.15M.
View Model Answer
a) Determining Rate Law: Compare Experiments 1 and 2 (B constant, A doubled): Rate doubles from 2.0×10⁻³ to 4.0×10⁻³ M/s. 4.0/2.0 = 2 = 2^m → m = 1. Reaction is first order in A. Compare Experiments 1 and 3 (A constant, B doubled): Rate quadruples from 2.0×10⁻³ to 8.0×10⁻³ M/s. 8.0/2.0 = 4 = 2^n → n = 2. Reaction is second order in B. Rate Law: Rate = k[A][B]² b) Calculate k using Experiment 1: Rate = k[A][B]² → 2.0×10⁻³ = k(0.10)(0.10)² = k(0.10)(0.01) = k(1.0×10⁻³). Therefore k = 2.0×10⁻³ / 1.0×10⁻³ = 2.0 M⁻² s⁻¹. Units: For a third-order reaction (1+2=3), rate = k[A][B]², units of k = M/s ÷ (M×M²) = M/s ÷ M³ = M⁻² s⁻¹. c) Predict rate: Rate = k[A][B]² = (2.0 M⁻² s⁻¹)(0.30 M)(0.15 M)² = (2.0)(0.30)(0.0225) = (2.0)(6.75×10⁻³) = 1.35×10⁻² M/s.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

a) Explain why the boiling point of H2O (100°C) is much higher than H2S (-60°C), even though sulfur has a much larger molar mass than oxygen. b) Explain why ionic compounds generally have much higher melting points than molecular compounds. c) Predict whether NaCl or MgO would have a higher melting point and explain your reasoning.
View Model Answer
a) H2O vs. H2S boiling points: Despite H2S having a larger molar mass (34 g/mol vs. 18 g/mol), water has a dramatically higher boiling point because oxygen's small size and high electronegativity (3.44) create a strong dipole, and each O-H bond can form hydrogen bonds with neighboring water molecules (O-H...O interactions, with energy ~20 kJ/mol). Water can form up to 4 hydrogen bonds per molecule, creating an extensive hydrogen-bonding network throughout the liquid that requires substantial energy to disrupt during vaporization. H2S lacks the ability to form true hydrogen bonds because sulfur's larger size and lower electronegativity (2.58) produce a weaker dipole and weaker S-H...S interactions. H2S primarily relies on weaker London dispersion forces and dipole-dipole interactions, which are overcome at much lower temperatures. b) Ionic compounds (lattice of alternating cations and anions held by electrostatic attraction) have much stronger interionic forces than the intermolecular forces in molecular compounds (London dispersion, dipole-dipole, H-bonding). The lattice energy of NaCl is ~788 kJ/mol. Melting an ionic compound requires breaking these strong electrostatic bonds throughout the entire crystal lattice simultaneously. Molecular compounds only need to overcome intermolecular forces (much weaker than ionic bonds) to melt the covalent bonds within molecules remain intact. Hence ionic compounds require much more thermal energy (higher temperature) to melt. c) MgO would have a higher melting point than NaCl. Both are ionic compounds, but the lattice energy (and thus melting point) depends on ion charge and ionic radius: Lattice energy is proportional to (q+×q-)/r. Mg²⁺ has charge +2 and O²⁻ has charge -2, so MgO has charge product = 4. Na⁺ has charge +1 and Cl⁻ has charge -1, so NaCl has charge product = 1. Additionally, Mg²⁺ and O²⁻ are smaller ions than Na⁺ and Cl⁻ respectively, further increasing lattice energy. MgO melting point ~2852°C vs. NaCl ~801°C consistent with MgO's much higher lattice energy from higher charges and smaller radii.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

For the reaction: N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇌ 2NH3(g), ΔH° = -92 kJ/mol a) Write the equilibrium constant expression Kc for this reaction. b) Predict how each of the following changes will affect the equilibrium position (shift left, right, or no change): (i) increasing pressure by decreasing volume, (ii) increasing temperature, (iii) adding a catalyst. c) The Haber process produces ammonia industrially. Explain why industrial conditions (typically 400-500°C and 150-300 atm) represent a compromise rather than the conditions predicted to give the highest yield.
View Model Answer
a) Kc = [NH3]² / ([N2][H2]³). This is the equilibrium constant expression: products over reactants, each raised to the power of their stoichiometric coefficient. Note: since all species are gases, this could also be expressed as Kp using partial pressures. b) (i) Increasing pressure by decreasing volume: shifts RIGHT. Le Chatelier's principle: the system responds to relieve the pressure increase by reducing the number of gas molecules. Reactant side has 1 + 3 = 4 moles of gas; product side has 2 moles of gas. Shifting toward products reduces moles of gas, relieving pressure. This is why the Haber process uses high pressure. (ii) Increasing temperature: shifts LEFT. The reaction is exothermic (ΔH° = -92 kJ/mol). Le Chatelier's principle treats the heat as a product: N2 + 3H2 ⇌ 2NH3 + heat. Adding heat (increasing temperature) shifts the equilibrium toward the endothermic direction (left), decomposing ammonia. Higher temperature decreases the equilibrium constant K and reduces ammonia yield. (iii) Adding a catalyst: NO CHANGE in equilibrium position. A catalyst lowers the activation energy for both forward and reverse reactions equally, increasing the rate at which equilibrium is reached but not changing the equilibrium constant or the equilibrium concentrations. It affects kinetics, not thermodynamics. c) The ideal conditions from equilibrium considerations would be: very high pressure (pushes equilibrium right) and very low temperature (exothermic reaction: lower T increases K and yield). However, the industrial compromise of 400-500°C is used because at very low temperatures, the reaction is extremely slow even with the iron catalyst it would take too long to reach equilibrium to be economically viable. The higher temperature (though reducing equilibrium yield) dramatically increases the reaction rate, making the process commercially feasible. Ammonia is continuously removed as it forms (Le Chatelier: shifts equilibrium right to replace removed product), and unreacted N2 and H2 are recycled. The 150-300 atm pressure is as high as economically practical higher pressures require extremely expensive, high-maintenance equipment that may not be cost-effective despite improving yield.

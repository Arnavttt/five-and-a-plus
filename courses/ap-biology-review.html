How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Bio is organized around four big ideas: Evolution, Cellular Processes (energy/communication), Genetics/Information Transfer, and Interactions. The exam heavily tests experimental design, data analysis, and application of concepts to novel scenarios. FRQs require using biological evidence to support claims description alone earns zero points. Mathematical skills (Hardy-Weinberg, chi-square, surface area to volume ratio) are tested.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students describe processes without explaining mechanisms. 'ATP is used for energy' earns no points 'ATP hydrolysis releases energy stored in the phosphate bond, which is used to power protein conformational changes in active transport' earns points. Students also confuse genotype ratios (3:1 for monohybrid cross) with phenotype ratios. In graph questions, students describe rather than interpret trends.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 student memorizes the steps of cellular processes. A 5 student understands why each step occurs mechanistically and can predict outcomes of experimental manipulations. Given 'what happens to cellular respiration if you add a proton pump inhibitor,' a 5 student reasons: 'proton gradient collapses → ATP synthase cannot function → chemiosmosis stops → only substrate-level phosphorylation produces ATP → net ATP drops drastically.'

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Focus on experimental design: practice identifying independent variables, dependent variables, controls, and potential confounds. Know Hardy-Weinberg calculations cold (p²+2pq+q²=1; p+q=1). Review the major metabolic pathways (glycolysis, Krebs, oxidative phosphorylation, photosynthesis) as flow diagrams with inputs/outputs. Do past FRQs and practice writing evidence-based claims.

Exam-Day Skills

  • FRQ claim-evidence-reasoning: Every FRQ response should follow: make a claim → provide specific biological evidence → explain the mechanistic reasoning linking evidence to claim. Never skip the reasoning step.
  • Graph interpretation: Describe the trend quantitatively (e.g., 'enzyme activity increases 3-fold from pH 4 to pH 7'), then interpret why biologically (optimal enzyme conformation at physiological pH).
  • Experimental design: Know the difference between a control group (receives no treatment or standard treatment) and a controlled variable (held constant). Identify what constitutes a valid control in AP Bio experiments.
  • Hardy-Weinberg: q² = frequency of homozygous recessive phenotype. Solve for q, then p. Calculate allele and genotype frequencies. Know the five conditions for H-W equilibrium.
  • MCQ elimination: Eliminate answers that describe the wrong biological level (cellular vs. organismal vs. population) or use incorrect directionality (e.g., enzyme activity increasing when substrate is absent).

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (50%): 60 MCQs in 90 min (including data analysis questions). Section II (50%): 6 FRQs in 90 min (2 long, 4 short).

Long FRQ (~8 to 10 pts): Requires designing an experiment, interpreting data, explaining biological mechanisms, and connecting to broader concepts. May include graphing or mathematical calculations (Hardy-Weinberg, chi-square, photosynthesis/respiration rates).

Short FRQ (~4 pts each): Usually two or three parts covering a single biological concept or process. May ask you to describe, explain, predict, or design.

Scoring principle: Every point requires specific biological evidence and mechanistic reasoning. 'Describe' questions require accurate descriptions of what happens. 'Explain' questions require WHY it happens. 'Predict' questions require stating the expected outcome AND the biological reason.

Data analysis: For graph interpretation questions: (1) accurately describe the quantitative trend with specific values from the graph, (2) interpret what the trend means biologically, (3) explain the mechanism that causes the trend. Never stop at description: interpretation and mechanism earn points.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. Which of the following best describes the structure of DNA?

  • A. Single-stranded helix with ribose sugar
  • B. Double helix with deoxyribose sugar and complementary base pairing
  • C. Single-stranded linear molecule
  • D. Double helix with ribose sugar and uracil
Show Answer

Answer: B: DNA is a double helix of two antiparallel strands with deoxyribose-phosphate backbone and nitrogenous bases (A-T, G-C) held by hydrogen bonds.

Q2. Natural selection acts most directly on:

  • A. Individual genotypes
  • B. Individual phenotypes (expressed traits)
  • C. Population gene frequencies directly
  • D. The genome as a whole
Show Answer

Answer: B: Natural selection acts on phenotypes (the expressed characteristics) individuals with adaptive phenotypes survive and reproduce more, indirectly changing allele frequencies over generations.

Q3. The process of cellular respiration produces ATP primarily through:

  • A. Fermentation in the cytoplasm
  • B. Oxidative phosphorylation in the mitochondrial inner membrane
  • C. Photosynthesis in chloroplasts
  • D. The Calvin cycle
Show Answer

Answer: B: The electron transport chain on the mitochondrial inner membrane drives ATP synthase through chemiosmosis (oxidative phosphorylation), producing ~32-34 ATP per glucose the majority of ATP from cellular respiration.

Q4. A protein's tertiary structure is determined by:

  • A. The sequence of amino acids (primary structure) alone
  • B. Interactions between R groups including hydrogen bonds, ionic bonds, disulfide bridges, and hydrophobic interactions
  • C. The number of subunits in the protein
  • D. The type of mRNA that coded for it
Show Answer

Answer: B: Tertiary structure: the 3D folding of a single polypeptide chain determined by R group interactions. Hydrophobic R groups fold away from water; charged R groups interact ionically; cysteines form disulfide bridges.

Q5. Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium requires which conditions?

  • A. Small population size and strong selection
  • B. Large population, random mating, no mutation, no selection, no gene flow
  • C. High mutation rates and genetic drift
  • D. Migration and natural selection
Show Answer

Answer: B: Hardy-Weinberg equilibrium is maintained in populations with: large size (no drift), random mating, no mutation, no selection, and no migration (gene flow). Real populations always deviate somewhat.

Q6. The sodium-potassium pump (Na+/K+ ATPase) is an example of:

  • A. Passive transport via channel proteins
  • B. Active transport using ATP to move ions against their concentration gradients
  • C. Facilitated diffusion
  • D. Osmosis
Show Answer

Answer: B: The Na+/K+ pump uses ATP to export 3 Na+ and import 2 K+ against their respective concentration gradients active transport maintaining the resting membrane potential.

Q7. In a dihybrid cross between two individuals heterozygous for both traits (AaBb x AaBb), what is the expected phenotypic ratio?

  • A. 3:1
  • B. 9:3:3:1
  • C. 1:1:1:1
  • D. 1:2:1
Show Answer

Answer: B: Mendel's law of independent assortment: dihybrid cross of AaBb x AaBb produces 9 A_B_ : 3 A_bb : 3 aaB_ : 1 aabb phenotypic ratio (assuming independent assortment and complete dominance).

Q8. Which type of RNA carries amino acids to the ribosome during translation?

  • A. mRNA (messenger RNA)
  • B. tRNA (transfer RNA)
  • C. rRNA (ribosomal RNA)
  • D. snRNA (small nuclear RNA)
Show Answer

Answer: B: tRNA is the adapter molecule: one end has an anticodon complementary to mRNA codons; the other end is aminoacylated (carries the specific amino acid). It delivers amino acids to the ribosome.

Q9. The primary function of the endoplasmic reticulum (rough ER) is:

  • A. Producing ATP through cellular respiration
  • B. Synthesizing, folding, and modifying proteins destined for secretion or membrane insertion
  • C. Storing calcium ions
  • D. Breaking down cellular waste
Show Answer

Answer: B: Rough ER (studded with ribosomes) synthesizes membrane and secretory proteins, folds them properly (with chaperone proteins), and performs initial glycosylation modifications.

Q10. In ecological terms, a 'keystone species' is one that:

  • A. Has the largest biomass in an ecosystem
  • B. Has a disproportionately large effect on ecosystem structure relative to its abundance
  • C. Occupies the top of the food chain
  • D. Produces the most energy in the ecosystem
Show Answer

Answer: B: Keystone species concept (Paine): some species have outsized ecological effects. Sea otters controlling sea urchins (which would otherwise destroy kelp forests) is a classic example.

Q11. Which of the following is a direct product of the light reactions of photosynthesis?

  • A. Glucose (C6H12O6)
  • B. ATP, NADPH, and O2
  • C. CO2 and H2O
  • D. 3-carbon sugar (G3P) only
Show Answer

Answer: B: Light reactions in thylakoid membranes produce: ATP (from photophosphorylation), NADPH (from NADP+ reduction), and O2 (from water splitting). These feed the Calvin cycle.

Q12. Crossing over (recombination) during meiosis is significant because it:

  • A. Doubles the number of chromosomes
  • B. Increases genetic variation by creating new combinations of alleles on chromosomes
  • C. Separates homologous chromosomes
  • D. Ensures identical genetic material in daughter cells
Show Answer

Answer: B: Crossing over during prophase I exchanges segments between homologous chromosomes, creating new allele combinations (recombinant chromosomes) and increasing the genetic diversity of offspring.

Q13. Which mechanism drives osmosis?

  • A. Active transport of water molecules by membrane pumps
  • B. Diffusion of water down its own concentration gradient (from high water potential to low)
  • C. Movement of solutes through aquaporins
  • D. ATP-powered movement of water against its gradient
Show Answer

Answer: B: Osmosis is the passive diffusion of water through a semipermeable membrane from an area of higher water potential (lower solute concentration) to lower water potential (higher solute concentration).

Q14. Feedback inhibition (allosteric inhibition) in enzyme pathways occurs when:

  • A. Substrate concentration is too low
  • B. The end product of a pathway inhibits an enzyme earlier in the pathway, regulating its own production
  • C. A competitive inhibitor blocks the active site
  • D. Temperature destroys the enzyme's tertiary structure
Show Answer

Answer: B: Feedback (end-product) inhibition: the end product of a metabolic pathway binds to an allosteric site on an early enzyme, changing its shape and reducing activity an elegant self-regulatory mechanism.

Q15. The lac operon in E. coli is an example of:

  • A. Eukaryotic gene regulation
  • B. Prokaryotic negative gene regulation in the absence of lactose, the repressor blocks transcription of lactose-metabolizing genes
  • C. Constitutive gene expression
  • D. Post-translational gene regulation
Show Answer

Answer: B: The lac operon: without lactose, a repressor protein binds the operator, blocking RNA polymerase from transcribing the structural genes. When lactose is present (as allolactose), it binds the repressor, releasing it from the operator, allowing transcription.

Q16. Which of the following would increase the rate of enzyme activity?

  • A. Adding a noncompetitive inhibitor
  • B. Increasing substrate concentration up to Vmax
  • C. Decreasing temperature toward 0°C
  • D. Changing pH far from the enzyme's optimum
Show Answer

Answer: B: Increasing substrate concentration increases reaction rate until all active sites are saturated (Vmax is reached). At Vmax, further increases in substrate concentration don't increase rate because all enzyme active sites are occupied.

Q17. The vertebrate immune system's adaptive immunity differs from innate immunity in that:

  • A. Innate immunity is faster and more specific
  • B. Adaptive immunity has specificity and immunological memory (mediated by B and T lymphocytes)
  • C. Adaptive immunity involves only phagocytes
  • D. Innate immunity requires prior exposure to pathogens
Show Answer

Answer: B: Adaptive immunity features: antigen-specific B and T lymphocytes that recognize specific antigens; clonal expansion; and immunological memory allowing faster, stronger secondary responses.

Q18. During aerobic cellular respiration, electrons are ultimately passed to:

  • A. NAD+ to form NADH
  • B. Oxygen, forming water
  • C. Carbon dioxide, forming CO2
  • D. The proton gradient across the inner mitochondrial membrane
Show Answer

Answer: B: The electron transport chain passes electrons through protein complexes, ultimately to O2 (the final electron acceptor), reducing it to water. This is why aerobic respiration requires oxygen.

Q19. Which of the following supports the endosymbiotic theory for the origin of mitochondria?

  • A. Mitochondria have no DNA
  • B. Mitochondria have their own circular DNA, double membrane, and ribosomes similar to bacteria
  • C. Mitochondria are found only in plant cells
  • D. Mitochondria reproduce by the same mechanism as the cell nucleus
Show Answer

Answer: C: Endosymbiotic theory (Lynn Margulis): mitochondria originated as free-living proteobacteria engulfed by ancestral eukaryotes. Evidence: circular mitochondrial DNA, double membranes, bacterial-size ribosomes, binary fission.

Q20. A population bottleneck leads to which evolutionary effect?

  • A. Increased genetic variation through natural selection
  • B. Reduced genetic diversity due to random loss of alleles in a small surviving population
  • C. Increased fitness of all surviving individuals
  • D. Speciation through geographic isolation
Show Answer

Answer: B: Bottleneck effect (a type of genetic drift): a catastrophic reduction in population size causes random loss of many alleles, greatly reducing genetic diversity. The surviving gene pool may not represent the original population.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

Enzyme activity was tested at different pH levels. At pH 2, activity was nearly zero. Activity rose to a maximum at pH 7, then declined sharply, reaching zero at pH 12. a) Explain why enzyme activity changes with pH, referencing the enzyme's molecular structure. b) Explain what happens to enzyme activity if the temperature is increased from 37°C to 100°C. c) Design an experiment to test whether the enzyme can be reactivated after being denatured by high temperature.
View Model Answer
a) Enzyme activity changes with pH because changes in hydrogen ion concentration ([H+]) alter the ionization state of the amino acid R groups at and around the active site. Many R groups contain charged groups (amino groups, carboxyl groups) whose charge depends on pH. At the enzyme's optimal pH (7 in this case), the R groups are in the correct ionization states to maintain the active site's precise 3D shape and to interact with the substrate via ionic bonds, hydrogen bonds, and other non-covalent interactions. At extreme pH values (pH 2 or pH 12), the excess H+ (acidic) or OH- (basic) ions disrupt the ionization state of R groups, breaking ionic bonds and hydrogen bonds that maintain tertiary structure. The active site changes shape, reducing its complementarity to the substrate. At very extreme pH values, the tertiary and secondary structure of the enzyme denatures the protein unfolds and loses all activity. This explains the bell-shaped activity curve observed. b) At 37°C (body temperature), the enzyme operates at or near its thermal optimum for most mammalian enzymes. Increasing temperature to 100°C would initially (at moderate increases) increase collision frequency and kinetic energy of substrate molecules, potentially increasing reaction rate. However, at 100°C far above the thermal stability of most biological proteins the intense thermal energy overwhelms the hydrogen bonds, hydrophobic interactions, and other weak forces maintaining tertiary structure. The enzyme denatures: the polypeptide chain unfolds chaotically, the active site loses its specific 3D shape, and enzyme activity drops to zero. Unlike the pH changes at moderate temperatures, heat denaturation at 100°C is typically irreversible. c) Experimental Design to test reactivation: Set up four tubes with identical amounts of purified enzyme. Tube 1 (control): maintain at 37°C throughout. Tube 2 (heat treatment): heat at 100°C for 10 minutes. Tube 3 (cooling test): heat at 100°C for 10 minutes, then cool to 37°C. Tube 4 (cooling + reducing agent test): heat at 100°C for 10 minutes, cool to 37°C, then add beta-mercaptoethanol (to break disulfide bonds and allow refolding). Add identical substrate to all tubes and measure product formation rate. Compare activity in Tube 3 vs. Tube 1 to test whether simple cooling restores activity. Compare Tube 4 to Tube 3 to test whether assisted refolding helps. Expected result: most mammalian enzymes at 100°C will not reactivate upon cooling denaturation is irreversible.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

a) Describe the process of DNA replication, including at least THREE enzymes involved and their roles. b) Explain how a mutation in the gene for DNA polymerase might affect an organism. c) Explain why DNA replication is described as 'semiconservative.'
View Model Answer
a) DNA Replication Process: Replication begins at specific sequences called origins of replication. Helicase unwinds and separates the double helix by breaking hydrogen bonds between base pairs, creating a replication fork. Single-strand binding proteins (SSBPs) stabilize the separated strands to prevent re-annealing. Primase synthesizes short RNA primers complementary to the template strand DNA polymerase cannot begin synthesis without a primer. DNA Polymerase III (in prokaryotes) / DNA Polymerase delta and epsilon (in eukaryotes) then adds nucleotides in the 5' to 3' direction, reading the template 3' to 5'. Because of this directionality, one strand (leading strand) is synthesized continuously; the other (lagging strand) is synthesized discontinuously as Okazaki fragments. DNA Ligase joins Okazaki fragments by forming phosphodiester bonds. DNA Polymerase I removes RNA primers and fills in the gaps. Topoisomerase relieves torsional stress ahead of the replication fork by cutting and rejoining DNA. b) A mutation in the DNA polymerase gene could affect the enzyme's proofreading function. DNA polymerase has 3' to 5' exonuclease activity that removes misincorporated nucleotides. If this proofreading domain is mutated, mismatched bases are not corrected during replication, resulting in a higher mutation rate throughout the genome. Over subsequent cell divisions, this accumulation of mutations could disrupt gene function, potentially inactivating tumor suppressor genes or activating oncogenes, increasing cancer risk. Alternatively, a mutation affecting the polymerase's processivity could cause frequent dissociation from the template, producing incomplete replications, stalled replication forks, and DNA double-strand breaks potentially lethal to the cell. c) DNA replication is semiconservative because each newly synthesized double helix consists of one original (parental) strand and one newly synthesized strand. When the double helix unwinds, each original strand serves as a template for synthesis of a new complementary strand. The result is two daughter molecules, each retaining half of the original molecule. This was demonstrated by Meselson and Stahl (1958) using nitrogen isotope labeling (15N/14N): after one replication in 14N medium, all DNA showed intermediate density (hybrid); after two replications, half showed intermediate and half showed light density consistent only with semiconservative replication.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

a) Explain how natural selection leads to evolution, using a specific example. b) Distinguish between directional, stabilizing, and disruptive selection. c) Explain ONE mechanism other than natural selection that can cause allele frequencies to change in a population.
View Model Answer
a) Natural selection leads to evolution through the following mechanism: A population shows heritable variation in traits (some individuals have slightly different phenotypes due to genetic differences). In any environment, resources are limited, so not all individuals survive to reproduce equally. Individuals with phenotypes better suited to the current environment survive longer and reproduce more successfully they have higher Darwinian fitness. Because the favored traits are heritable, the next generation contains more alleles associated with the adaptive traits and fewer associated with less adaptive traits. Over many generations, the cumulative change in allele frequencies constitutes evolution. Example: Antibiotic resistance in bacteria. When antibiotics are applied, the rare individuals with resistance alleles (through prior random mutation) survive and reproduce. Susceptible individuals die. After many generations of selection, the population is dominated by resistant alleles the population has evolved resistance through natural selection. b) Directional selection shifts the mean phenotype in one direction by favoring individuals at one extreme of the phenotypic distribution e.g., favoring larger body size in a cold climate, the population average shifts toward larger size over time. Stabilizing selection favors intermediate phenotypes and acts against both extremes e.g., human birth weight: very small or very large babies have higher mortality, selecting for intermediate weight. This maintains existing phenotypes and reduces variation. Disruptive (diversifying) selection favors both extreme phenotypes over the intermediate e.g., black and white color morphs of a bird are both camouflaged in their respective microhabitats while intermediate gray birds are visible to predators in both environments. This can lead to bimodal phenotypic distributions and potentially speciation. c) Genetic drift: random changes in allele frequencies due to chance events, particularly in small populations. Genetic drift is especially powerful when population size is reduced dramatically (bottleneck effect) or when a small group colonizes a new area (founder effect). Unlike natural selection, drift is random and not adaptive it can cause harmful alleles to become fixed or beneficial alleles to be lost purely by chance. Example: The founder effect in Amish populations of Pennsylvania has produced high frequencies of certain rare genetic disorders (Ellis-van Creveld syndrome) because the founding group happened to carry these alleles, and the isolated community's genetic makeup reflects the founders' gene pool, not the broader human population.

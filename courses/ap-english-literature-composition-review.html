How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Lit tests literary analysis of prose fiction, poetry, and longer works across three FRQ types: Poetry Analysis, Prose Fiction Analysis, and Literary Argument (about a work of your choice). MCQs test close reading of poetry and prose identifying literary devices, their effects, shifts in tone, speaker's attitude, and narrative technique. About 55% of the exam tests poetry practice it more than prose.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students identify literary devices without analyzing their function in the text. 'The author uses imagery' earns no credit 'The author's water imagery shifts from stagnation (the 'still pond' of the opening) to turbulence ('the river floods its banks') as the speaker's grief transforms into action, mirroring the psychological arc of the poem' earns full credit. In prose FRQs, students retell the plot instead of analyzing the author's craft.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 writer can identify devices and their definitions. A 5 writer reads the text as a unified aesthetic object they see how diction, syntax, imagery, structure, and tone work together to produce a complex meaning. The Literary Argument FRQ requires selecting and applying a novel, play, or long poem with sufficient literary complexity a student who has read 5 to 8 worthy texts deeply outperforms one who has read 20 texts superficially.

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Read 3 to 5 poems per day using TPCASTT (Title, Paraphrase, Connotation, Attitude, Shifts, Theme, Title revisit). Write a complete poetry FRQ essay in 40 minutes at least 3 times before the exam. Build your Literary Argument repertoire: prepare at least 3 to 4 works with specific passage knowledge, thematic analyses, and ready arguments. Review narrative technique vocabulary (free indirect discourse, unreliable narrator, in medias res).

Exam-Day Skills

  • Poetry FRQ thesis: Identify the poem's central tension or complexity and the primary literary techniques the author uses to develop it. Example: 'In "The Love Song of J. Alfred Prufrock," Eliot uses fragmented syntax, ironic allusion, and the dramatic monologue form to dramatize the paralysis of a modern consciousness unable to act in a world that has lost coherent meaning.'
  • Literary device analysis: Always: Identify → Quote (briefly) → Analyze effect → Connect to larger meaning. Never stop at identification.
  • Prose FRQ focus: Analyze the narrator's perspective and technique. How does narrative distance (close third person vs. omniscient) shape what the reader knows and feels? How does syntax (long periods vs. short declarative sentences) reflect the character's mental state?
  • Literary Argument FRQ: Choose your work strategically select one that fits the prompt broadly enough that you can write a full argument, not just a summary. Know 3 to 5 specific scenes or passages with textual evidence.
  • MCQ poetry approach: Read the poem through twice before answering once for sense, once for technique. Mark tone shifts, speaker changes, and structural turns (volta in sonnets, section breaks).

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (45%): 55 MCQs in 60 min (reading short fiction, prose passages, and poems, then answering close reading questions). Section II (55%): 3 FRQs in 120 min (~40 min each).

FRQ 1: Poetry Analysis (6 pts): Analyze how literary elements and techniques in a poem contribute to its meaning. Scored on: Thesis identifying a claim about the poem's meaning (1 pt), Evidence & Commentary with textual support and analysis of literary technique effects (4 pts on a scale), Sophistication (1 pt).

FRQ 2: Prose Fiction Analysis (6 pts): Analyze how an author's choices in a prose passage create a specific effect (characterization, narrative technique, thematic development). Same rubric as Poetry FRQ.

FRQ 3: Literary Argument (6 pts): Choose a work of literary merit and develop an argument about how a specific element contributes to its meaning. Same rubric, but you supply both the text and the argument. Must choose a work of sufficient literary complexity: choose wisely based on your knowledge depth.

Evidence & Commentary scoring: The same 0 to 4 scale applies to all three essays. A score of 4 requires specific textual evidence (quoted or precisely referenced) AND thorough analysis explaining how the evidence illustrates the literary claim and contributes to the poem's/work's meaning.

Sophistication (1 pt): Earned by complexity: showing how multiple literary techniques work together, placing the work in a broader literary or cultural context, or acknowledging tensions within the text.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. In literary analysis, 'theme' refers to:

  • A. The setting and time period of a literary work
  • B. A central insight about human experience that the work explores or develops
  • C. The chronological sequence of events in a plot
  • D. The biographical background of the author
Show Answer

Answer: B: Theme: the abstract insight, claim, or question about human experience a literary work develops not a topic ('love') but a statement about the topic ('love requires vulnerability that makes loss inevitable').

Q2. 'Dramatic irony' occurs when:

  • A. A character says the opposite of what they mean
  • B. The audience knows something important that one or more characters do not
  • C. The outcome is the opposite of what is expected
  • D. Two characters have opposite opinions about the same situation
Show Answer

Answer: B: Dramatic irony: the audience possesses knowledge unavailable to a character e.g., Oedipus seeking his father's killer while the audience knows he is that killer. Creates tension, pathos, or tragic effect.

Q3. The term 'foil' in literature describes:

  • A. The climactic moment of a story
  • B. A character who contrasts with another (usually the protagonist) to highlight the protagonist's qualities
  • C. The narrative voice of a story
  • D. A symbol that appears repeatedly throughout a work
Show Answer

Answer: B: A foil character: through contrast, reveals qualities of the character they're paired with. Laertes' impulsive action makes Hamlet's paralysis more visible; Iago's cynical realism against Othello's idealism highlights both characters' distinctive qualities.

Q4. Free verse poetry differs from formal verse in that:

  • A. It has no imagery or figurative language
  • B. It does not follow a fixed metrical pattern or rhyme scheme, though it may use other organizing principles
  • C. It is always prose arranged on a page
  • D. It cannot express complex emotions
Show Answer

Answer: B: Free verse abandons fixed meter and rhyme but uses other organizational strategies line breaks, repetition, rhythm, sound patterns, white space to create meaning. Whitman, Ginsberg, and most contemporary poetry use free verse.

Q5. The narrative technique of 'in medias res' means:

  • A. The story is told in strict chronological order
  • B. The story begins in the middle of the action, before filling in background through flashback or exposition
  • C. The narrator knows all characters' thoughts
  • D. The story is told from multiple perspectives simultaneously
Show Answer

Answer: B: In medias res (Latin: in the middle of things): the classic epic convention of beginning in the middle of the action (Homer's Iliad begins with the anger of Achilles, well into the Trojan War) rather than at the chronological beginning.

Q6. Which of the following best defines 'allegory'?

  • A. A brief illustrative story with a moral lesson (fable)
  • B. A narrative in which characters, events, and settings represent abstract ideas or moral/political qualities beyond the literal level
  • C. The use of exaggeration for comic effect
  • D. A poem addressed to a person, place, or abstract concept
Show Answer

Answer: B: Allegory: a narrative with two simultaneous levels of meaning the literal story and an abstract meaning. Orwell's Animal Farm is literally a story about farm animals, allegorically a critique of Soviet totalitarianism.

Q7. 'Stream of consciousness' as a narrative technique:

  • A. Presents events in strict chronological sequence
  • B. Attempts to replicate the continuous, associative flow of a character's thoughts and perceptions without the imposition of logical order
  • C. Uses an omniscient narrator who comments on events
  • D. Presents multiple narrators in alternating chapters
Show Answer

Answer: B: Stream of consciousness (Woolf, Joyce, Faulkner): mimics the fluid, associative, often non-linear flow of thought memories intruding on present, sensory details triggering associations, thoughts incomplete and interrupted.

Q8. The term 'pathos' in literary criticism refers to:

  • A. A logical argument based on evidence
  • B. The quality in a text that evokes pity, sadness, or compassion in the reader
  • C. The use of humor and comic relief
  • D. The ethical dimension of a character's choices
Show Answer

Answer: B: Pathos: the quality that evokes emotional response pity, sadness, compassion. In tragedy, the suffering of a character with whom we identify creates the pathos central to catharsis. Aristotle identified pathos as one of tragedy's emotional purposes.

Q9. An 'unreliable narrator' is one who:

  • A. Tells the story in first person
  • B. Provides an account that the reader has reason to distrust, due to self-deception, limited knowledge, psychological instability, or dishonesty
  • C. Knows only what can be directly observed
  • D. Tells the story from outside the events described
Show Answer

Answer: B: Unreliable narrator: the reader recognizes gaps, contradictions, or self-serving distortions in the narrator's account. Stevens in Ishiguro's Remains of the Day, Humbert Humbert in Lolita, and the narrator of The Yellow Wallpaper are classic examples.

Q10. Blank verse is defined as:

  • A. Poetry with no rhyme scheme or meter
  • B. Unrhymed iambic pentameter
  • C. Poetry with a strict ABAB rhyme scheme
  • D. A prose poem without line breaks
Show Answer

Answer: B: Blank verse: unrhymed lines in iambic pentameter (10 syllables, alternating unstressed/stressed). Shakespeare's dramatic speeches and Milton's Paradise Lost are written primarily in blank verse formal enough for elevated diction, flexible enough for natural speech rhythms.

Q11. The term 'catharsis' as Aristotle used it in relation to tragedy refers to:

  • A. The tragic hero's fatal flaw
  • B. The emotional purging or release pity and fear that tragedy produces in the audience
  • C. The reversal of fortune experienced by the protagonist
  • D. The moment of recognition (anagnorisis) in a tragic plot
Show Answer

Answer: B: Aristotle's Poetics: tragedy produces catharsis a purgation or clarification of the emotions of pity (for the suffering hero) and fear (that such suffering could befall us) through the work of art.

Q12. 'Motif' in literary analysis refers to:

  • A. The central message of a work
  • B. A recurring element image, symbol, idea, phrase that develops the work's themes
  • C. The exposition that introduces setting and characters
  • D. The resolution of the central conflict
Show Answer

Answer: B: Motif: a recurring element that carries thematic significance through repetition and variation. The green light in The Great Gatsby, the albatross in The Rime of the Ancient Mariner, or water imagery in Beloved are motifs each appearance adds cumulative meaning.

Q13. The distinction between 'simile' and 'metaphor' is that:

  • A. Similes compare unlike things; metaphors compare like things
  • B. Similes use 'like' or 'as' to make explicit comparisons; metaphors assert identity directly
  • C. Metaphors are always more powerful than similes
  • D. Similes are only used in poetry; metaphors appear in prose
Show Answer

Answer: B: Simile: 'She is like a summer's day.' Metaphor: 'She is a summer's day.' The metaphor's assertion of identity (rather than similarity) creates a more complete, less qualified identification between tenor and vehicle.

Q14. 'Hamartia' in Greek tragedy refers to:

  • A. The moment of greatest dramatic tension
  • B. The protagonist's fatal flaw or error that contributes to their downfall
  • C. The chorus's commentary on the action
  • D. The divine forces that determine human fate
Show Answer

Answer: B: Hamartia (Aristotle): the fatal flaw, error of judgment, or mistaken action of the tragic hero that initiates the sequence leading to downfall. Oedipus' hamartia is his determination to know the truth; Othello's is jealousy; Macbeth's is ambition.

Q15. The 'volta' in a Shakespearean (English) sonnet occurs:

  • A. At the beginning of the third quatrain
  • B. At line 9, or more typically at the couplet (lines 13-14), where the poem pivots or shifts in thought
  • C. After the first quatrain
  • D. In the middle of the poem
Show Answer

Answer: B: Shakespearean sonnet: three quatrains (ABAB CDCD EFEF) plus a couplet (GG). The volta (turn) typically occurs at the couplet a resolution, ironic reversal, or complication of what the quatrains established.

Q16. Which of the following best describes 'magical realism'?

  • A. Fantasy fiction set in invented worlds
  • B. A narrative mode where magical or supernatural elements appear in an otherwise realistic setting without authorial comment on their impossibility
  • C. Science fiction exploring future technologies
  • D. Horror fiction relying on supernatural entities
Show Answer

Answer: B: Magical realism (García Márquez, Allende, Toni Morrison): supernatural events are presented matter-of-factly within realistic settings, reflecting cultural worldviews where the supernatural is continuous with reality rather than separate from it.

Q17. The term 'deus ex machina' refers to:

  • A. A metaphor drawn from machinery
  • B. An abrupt, contrived resolution to a story through an unlikely external agent or event
  • C. The use of technology as a symbol in modern literature
  • D. A god-figure who serves as protagonist
Show Answer

Answer: B: Deus ex machina (god from the machine): an artificially imposed resolution in which an outside force suddenly solves the seemingly insoluble problem originally Greek theatrical cranes lowering actors playing gods. Aristotle criticized it as a failure of plot construction.

Q18. 'Polyphony' (Bakhtin) in the novel describes:

  • A. The use of multiple narrators telling the same story
  • B. A narrative where multiple voices, perspectives, and ideologies coexist without being resolved by the author's authoritative voice
  • C. Poetry that imitates musical forms
  • D. The use of dialect to distinguish characters
Show Answer

Answer: B: Bakhtin's polyphonic novel: in Dostoevsky, characters speak with full autonomy their voices are not subordinated to or judged by the author's perspective. Each consciousness is given its own weight, creating ideological dialogue rather than monologue.

Q19. 'Synecdoche' is a figure of speech in which:

  • A. An abstract idea is represented by a concrete image
  • B. A part represents the whole or the whole represents a part
  • C. Two contradictory terms are combined for effect
  • D. An absent person is directly addressed
Show Answer

Answer: B: Synecdoche: part for whole ('All hands on deck' hands = sailors) or whole for part ('America won the gold medal' America = a specific American athlete). Related to metonymy but specifically involves part-whole relationships.

Q20. The AP Literature essay's 'literary argument' requires:

  • A. Summarizing the plot and identifying literary devices
  • B. Developing a debatable interpretive claim about a work's meaning and supporting it with textual evidence and literary analysis
  • C. Comparing the work to the author's life
  • D. Evaluating whether the work is good or bad
Show Answer

Answer: B: A literary argument requires a debatable, interpretive thesis (not just 'Shakespeare uses metaphor' but 'Shakespeare's metaphors of disease and corruption construct Denmark as a body politic that Hamlet both cannot and must purge') supported by close reading and analysis of how specific literary choices create meaning.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (Poetry Analysis FRQ)

Read the following poem carefully. Then write a well-developed essay analyzing how the poet uses literary devices to develop a complex theme. 'Those Winter Sundays' by Robert Hayden (1966) Sundays too my father got up early and put his clothes on in the blueblack cold, then with cracked hands that ached from labor in the weekday weather made banked fires blaze. No one ever thanked him. I'd wake and hear the cold splintering, breaking. When the rooms were warm, he'd call, and slowly I would rise and dress, fearing the chronic angers of that house. Speaking indifferently to him, who had driven out the cold and polished my good shoes as well. What did I know, what did I know of love's austere and lonely offices?
View Model Answer
THESIS: In 'Those Winter Sundays,' Hayden uses tactile and sonic imagery, subtle tonal shift, and the retrospective interrogative to develop a meditation on how love can be expressed through unremarked labor and how such love may only be recognized with guilt after the opportunity for reciprocation has passed. IMAGERY AND DICTION: The poem's opening images are sensory and harsh 'blueblack cold,' 'cracked hands that ached,' 'labor in the weekday weather.' These tactile images make the father's sacrifice physically present; the reader feels the cold and the pain before any emotional claim is made. 'Blueblack' fuses coldness with darkness and subtly bruising, suggesting a quality of suffering in the father's life that the child never registered. The father 'made / banked fires blaze' the enjambment and active verb 'made' give him agency and power; he is not defeated by the cold but transforms it. Yet 'No one ever thanked him' the flat declarative after the warmth of the fire is devastating in its simplicity. STRUCTURE AND TONAL SHIFT: The poem shifts from past description (what happened) to present retrospect (what the speaker now understands). 'Fearing the chronic angers of that house' introduces ambiguity whose anger? The father's? The general domestic tension? The child's misreading of the father's severity as anger rather than exhaustion? The repetition 'What did I know, what did I know' performs the speaker's current self-reproach the doubled question mimics the speaker rehearsing the accusation against himself, the repetition adding grief and emphasis. THEME AND 'LOVE'S AUSTERE AND LONELY OFFICES': The poem's final line is its interpretive key. 'Offices' carries religious connotation the Divine Office, ritual duties performed whether witnessed or not. 'Austere and lonely': love that demands no return, asks for no recognition, is performed in the dark before the household wakes. The poem argues that love may be most fully expressed not in warmth and words but in the unglamorous, unwitnessed acts of service and that this is precisely the form least likely to be recognized by its beneficiaries until too late.

FRQ 2 (Prose Fiction Analysis FRQ)

The following passage is from Toni Morrison's Beloved (1987). Read it carefully, then write an essay analyzing how Morrison uses narrative technique, diction, and imagery to develop her central concerns. [Passage excerpt context: Sethe, a formerly enslaved woman, has just encountered the woman she believes is the ghost of her dead daughter, Beloved. She reflects on memory.] 'Places, places are still there, and what's more, if you go there and stand in the place where it was, it will happen again; it will be there waiting for you. So, Denver, you can't never go back. Never. Because even though it's all over over and done with it's going to always be there waiting for you.' Analyze how Morrison's craft choices in this passage and throughout your reading of the novel develop her exploration of trauma and memory.
View Model Answer
THESIS: Through Sethe's theory of 'rememory,' Morrison uses deceptively simple diction, spatial metaphor, and the paradox of past-as-present to develop her central claim that trauma is not past but physically immanent that for survivors of slavery, the past is not over but an ongoing, spatially real presence that can reach out and possess the living. NARRATIVE TECHNIQUE: INTERIORITY AND DIRECT ADDRESS: The passage is delivered as Sethe's direct speech to Denver, creating intimacy and immediacy. Morrison's frequent use of the present tense ('it will be there,' 'it's going to always be') where we expect the past tense performs the novel's central argument: traumatic memory exists in a present continuous, not a simple past. The repetition of 'Never. Because even though it's all over over and done with it's going to always be' mimics the way trauma intrudes compulsively, the speaker circling back to the same impossible fact. DICTION: SIMPLICITY AS STRATEGY: Morrison uses grammatically simple, vernacular language ('you can't never go back,' 'what's more') that carries extraordinary philosophical weight. 'Rememory' is Morrison's coined portmanteau re + memory suggesting memory that recurs, that comes back, that is re-experienced rather than merely recalled. The vernacular diction is not a limitation but a precision: this is not psychological abstraction but embodied, lived knowledge. SPATIAL METAPHOR: PLACE AS WOUND: The passage insists on the spatial reality of traumatic memory. Places 'still there,' 'stand in the place where it was,' 'waiting for you' memory is not in the mind but in the landscape, waiting for the unwary. This spatial externalization of trauma reflects the novel's larger project: Beloved herself is a spatial, bodily, persistent memory made flesh the past literally returning in physical form. The haunted house at 124 Bluestone Road embodies the same principle: trauma occupies space, makes space uninhabitable, until it is confronted. HISTORICAL DIMENSION: Morrison's formal choices serve her historical argument: the trauma of slavery cannot be 'gotten over' by individual will because it is systemic and ongoing. The spatial metaphor of rememory places waiting to re-enact the past on any body that enters them speaks to how institutional racism perpetuates historical violence: the 'place' of slavery's structures persists, waiting for descendants to enter and re-experience its effects. The novel refuses consoling narratives of transcendence.

FRQ 3 (Open Literary Argument FRQ)

Many works of literature explore the tension between individual desire and social obligation. Choose a novel, play, or epic poem in which a character faces this conflict. Then write a well-developed essay arguing how this tension illuminates a complex theme the work develops. Do not merely summarize the plot.
View Model Answer
[Model essay using Sophocles' Antigone] THESIS: In Sophocles' Antigone, the irresolvable conflict between Antigone's religiously-grounded filial obligation and Creon's politically-grounded civic authority illuminates the tragedy's central insight: that in truly tragic situations, both sides of a conflict may embody genuine moral goods, and the catastrophe results not from one party's evil but from the mutual incompatibility of legitimate claims a structural tragedy that reveals the limits of human law and the cost of moral certainty. ANTIGONE'S CLAIM: Antigone's decision to bury her brother Polynices against Creon's edict rests on a claim that divine law (the gods' requirement that the dead receive burial rites) supersedes human positive law. Her position is not mere sentiment but a coherent theological-ethical argument: 'Nor did I think your edicts had such force / that you, a mortal man, could override / the gods' unwritten and unfailing laws.' Antigone's courage is genuine; her love for her brother is genuine; her theological claim has real weight in the Greek moral universe. Her hamartia if she has one is inflexibility, the inability to bend. CREON'S CLAIM: Creon's position is also defensible: a newly crowned king, following a civil war in which Polynices attacked Thebes, must establish that civic loyalty takes precedence over family loyalty. The state cannot function if private obligations routinely override public law. His claim that the traitor Polynices should not receive the honors due a patriot reflects a coherent political theory. His error is not the principle but its application with unmovable rigidity and crucially his conflation of his personal authority with the city's interest. HEGELIAN TRAGEDY: Hegel read Antigone as the paradigmatic case of a tragedy where two legitimate ethical claims collide family (represented by Antigone) versus state (Creon) and both are partially right. The catastrophe destroys both parties (Antigone, Haemon, Eurydice) precisely because neither can accommodate the other's genuine claim. This mutual destruction reveals the tragedy's theme: that the moral universe contains competing legitimate goods, and hubris for Sophocles is the certainty that one's own claim is total and the other's is nothing. CONCLUSION: The play does not endorse Antigone's position over Creon's or vice versa. Tiresias' warning applies to both: when human beings mistake their partial perspective for absolute truth, they call down destruction. The tension between individual obligation and social authority is not resolved by Antigone's death it is shown to be irresolvable within the constraints of human finitude and the competing demands of an ethically complex world.

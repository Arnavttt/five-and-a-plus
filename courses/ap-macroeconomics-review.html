How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Macro emphasizes the circular flow model, national income accounting, aggregate demand/supply model (AD-AS), fiscal policy, monetary policy, the money market, loanable funds market, foreign exchange market, and balance of payments. Graphs are the language of AP Macro roughly 50% of FRQ points require drawing and manipulating economic graphs correctly.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students lose FRQ points by shifting the wrong curve. If government spending increases, shift AD right not SRAS. Students confuse expansionary vs. contractionary monetary policy directions. Labeling graphs incorrectly (wrong axes, missing labels, curves not intersecting properly) loses points mechanically. Students also confuse nominal and real GDP.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 student knows policy prescriptions. A 5 student can trace a complete policy transmission: 'Fed buys bonds → money supply increases → interest rates fall → investment spending increases → AD shifts right → real GDP rises and price level rises in the short run → in the long run, SRAS shifts left as input prices rise, returning output to potential.' Complete chain reasoning earns full credit.

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Draw and label every major graph from memory: AD-AS, money market, loanable funds, Phillips curve, production possibilities curve, foreign exchange. For each graph, practice all possible shifts and their effects. Memorize all policy prescriptions for recession vs. inflation scenarios. Do 2 FRQ sets per week timing yourself at 10 min per question.

Exam-Day Skills

  • Graph drawing protocol: Every graph needs: labeled axes (Price Level / Real GDP, or Interest Rate / Quantity of Money, etc.), a downward-sloping or upward-sloping curve, intersection point labeled with P* and Q*, and arrow showing the shift direction with the new equilibrium labeled.
  • Policy chain reasoning: Always trace effects to the final economic variables. Don't stop at 'interest rates fall' continue to investment, AD, output, price level, and unemployment.
  • MCQ strategy: Many MCQs give a scenario and ask what happens to two variables (e.g., 'real GDP and price level'). Draw a quick graph in your test booklet before selecting the answer.
  • Foreign exchange: Know that U.S. interest rates rising causes capital inflow → demand for dollars rises → dollar appreciates → exports fall, imports rise. Practice the full chain both directions.
  • Crowding out: Fiscal policy stimulus → government borrowing increases → loanable funds demand rises → interest rates rise → private investment falls. Know this chain cold.

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (66%): 60 MCQs in 70 min. Section II (34%): 3 FRQs in 60 min (1 long FRQ ~23 min + 2 short FRQs ~17 min each).

Long FRQ (~10 pts): Typically requires drawing and correctly labeling 2 to 3 economic graphs (AD-AS, money market, loanable funds, foreign exchange) and tracing the effects of a policy through the economy. Each correctly drawn and labeled graph = pts; each correctly traced effect = pts.

Short FRQs (~5 pts each): Focus on a specific market or concept. May ask you to draw one graph, identify the direction of a shift, state the effect on a variable (real GDP, price level, interest rate, exchange rate), and explain why.

Graph labeling requirements: Axes must be labeled (e.g., 'Real GDP' not 'output'; 'Price Level' not 'prices'). Curves must be labeled (AD, SRAS, LRAS, MS, MD, etc.). Equilibrium points must be labeled (P*, Q*, or r*, Q*). Shifts must show new equilibrium with subscript (P₁, Q₁).

Chain reasoning: For any policy, trace effects through ALL affected markets in sequence. Graders award points for each correctly identified step in the chain.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. GDP measures:

  • A. The total value of a country's imports
  • B. The total market value of all final goods and services produced within a country's borders in a given year
  • C. The total income of a country's citizens wherever they live
  • D. The total value of all transactions in the economy
Show Answer

Answer: B: GDP (Gross Domestic Product) counts final goods and services produced domestically, avoiding double-counting by excluding intermediate goods.

Q2. An increase in the money supply, holding other things equal, will in the long run most likely cause:

  • A. An increase in real GDP
  • B. Inflation, with no lasting effect on real output
  • C. A decrease in interest rates permanently
  • D. An increase in real wages
Show Answer

Answer: B: The quantity theory of money (MV=PQ): in the long run, money is neutral increases in money supply raise price levels (inflation) without affecting real output or employment.

Q3. Which of the following would shift the aggregate demand curve to the right?

  • A. An increase in taxes
  • B. A decrease in government spending
  • C. An increase in consumer confidence
  • D. An appreciation of the domestic currency
Show Answer

Answer: C: Increased consumer confidence raises consumption (C), a component of AD = C + I + G + NX, shifting the AD curve rightward.

Q4. The multiplier effect in Keynesian economics refers to:

  • A. The amplifying effect of money supply increases on inflation
  • B. The phenomenon where an initial change in spending leads to a larger change in equilibrium GDP
  • C. The impact of monetary policy on interest rates
  • D. The relationship between government deficits and national debt
Show Answer

Answer: B: The spending multiplier = 1/(1-MPC). An initial injection of spending is re-spent through the economy, generating a larger total increase in GDP.

Q5. A country running a trade deficit is:

  • A. Always in economic trouble
  • B. Importing more goods and services than it is exporting
  • C. Exporting more than it imports
  • D. Running a government budget deficit
Show Answer

Answer: B: A trade (current account) deficit means imports exceed exports (NX < 0), meaning the country is borrowing from abroad or selling assets to finance the excess imports.

Q6. The natural rate of unemployment includes:

  • A. Cyclical unemployment only
  • B. Frictional and structural unemployment, excluding cyclical unemployment
  • C. All unemployed workers during a recession
  • D. Workers who have stopped looking for work
Show Answer

Answer: B: The natural rate = frictional (job-searching) + structural (skills mismatch) unemployment. Cyclical unemployment (from recession) is the gap between actual and natural rate.

Q7. According to the loanable funds model, an increase in government borrowing will:

  • A. Decrease interest rates
  • B. Increase real interest rates and crowd out private investment
  • C. Decrease national saving
  • D. Have no effect on the private sector
Show Answer

Answer: B: Increased government borrowing raises demand for loanable funds, driving up real interest rates and reducing private investment the crowding-out effect.

Q8. The Consumer Price Index (CPI) measures:

  • A. The rate of economic growth
  • B. The average change in prices paid by urban consumers for a basket of goods over time
  • C. Corporate price-setting behavior
  • D. The cost of living for all Americans
Show Answer

Answer: B: The CPI tracks price changes for a fixed basket of goods representative of typical urban consumer purchases, used to measure inflation and adjust wages/benefits.

Q9. Contractionary monetary policy is most appropriate when:

  • A. Unemployment is high
  • B. Inflation is above target and the economy is overheating
  • C. The economy is in recession
  • D. Interest rates are already very high
Show Answer

Answer: B: The Fed raises the federal funds rate (contractionary) when inflation exceeds target, slowing spending, reducing AD, and bringing price level back toward target.

Q10. The Phillips curve represents the short-run tradeoff between:

  • A. Inflation and economic growth
  • B. Unemployment and inflation
  • C. Interest rates and investment
  • D. Government spending and GDP
Show Answer

Answer: B: The short-run Phillips curve shows an inverse relationship between unemployment and inflation lower unemployment typically comes with higher inflation, and vice versa.

Q11. Stagflation is characterized by:

  • A. High growth and high inflation
  • B. Simultaneous high inflation and high unemployment
  • C. High unemployment and deflation
  • D. Low growth and low inflation
Show Answer

Answer: B: Stagflation (1970s) combined high inflation with high unemployment defying the simple Phillips curve trade-off and forcing reconsideration of Keynesian demand management.

Q12. An expansionary fiscal policy would include:

  • A. Raising taxes and reducing government spending
  • B. Cutting taxes and increasing government spending
  • C. Raising interest rates
  • D. Selling government bonds in the open market
Show Answer

Answer: B: Expansionary fiscal policy increases AD by cutting taxes (boosting consumption) or increasing government spending (directly adding to AD).

Q13. In the long run, the aggregate supply curve is:

  • A. Upward sloping like the short-run AS curve
  • B. Vertical at the full-employment (potential) GDP level
  • C. Downward sloping
  • D. Horizontal at the current price level
Show Answer

Answer: B: Long-run AS is vertical at potential GDP because in the long run, wages and prices are fully flexible real output is determined by factors of production, not price level.

Q14. Open market operations refer to:

  • A. Government spending in international markets
  • B. The Federal Reserve buying or selling U.S. Treasury securities to influence the money supply
  • C. The Treasury Department selling new bonds to finance deficits
  • D. Banks making loans to the public
Show Answer

Answer: B: The Fed's primary monetary policy tool: buying securities injects reserves (expansionary); selling securities removes reserves (contractionary).

Q15. The term 'real interest rate' refers to:

  • A. The federal funds rate set by the Fed
  • B. The nominal interest rate minus the expected inflation rate
  • C. Interest rates on 10-year Treasury bonds
  • D. The rate banks charge each other for overnight loans
Show Answer

Answer: B: Real interest rate = nominal rate - inflation rate (Fisher equation). It represents the true cost of borrowing in terms of purchasing power.

Q16. A budget surplus occurs when:

  • A. Government tax revenues exceed government expenditures
  • B. Government expenditures exceed tax revenues
  • C. The trade balance is positive
  • D. Money supply grows faster than GDP
Show Answer

Answer: A: A budget surplus exists when government revenues (taxes) exceed expenditures in a given fiscal year the government collects more than it spends.

Q17. Frictional unemployment results from:

  • A. Declining industries shedding workers
  • B. Workers voluntarily between jobs or searching for better employment
  • C. Recessions reducing overall demand for labor
  • D. Automation replacing human workers
Show Answer

Answer: B: Frictional unemployment is temporary and voluntary workers searching for better jobs, new graduates seeking first employment, or workers changing careers. It's natural and even healthy.

Q18. Which policy tool would most effectively address demand-pull inflation?

  • A. Increasing government spending
  • B. Raising the reserve requirement to reduce money supply and credit
  • C. Cutting corporate tax rates
  • D. Increasing transfer payments to households
Show Answer

Answer: B: Demand-pull inflation (too much money chasing too few goods) requires contractionary policy: raising reserve requirements reduces bank lending, shrinking money supply and cooling demand.

Q19. In the Keynesian cross model, equilibrium occurs when:

  • A. The price level equals the wage level
  • B. Planned expenditures equal actual output (GDP)
  • C. Aggregate supply equals aggregate demand at the full employment level
  • D. The government budget is balanced
Show Answer

Answer: B: Keynesian equilibrium: the economy reaches equilibrium where planned (intended) aggregate expenditure equals actual output there's no unintended inventory accumulation or depletion.

Q20. Structural unemployment is best addressed by:

  • A. Expansionary fiscal policy to boost demand
  • B. Job retraining, education, and skills development programs
  • C. Lower interest rates to stimulate investment
  • D. Reducing the minimum wage
Show Answer

Answer: B: Structural unemployment reflects skills mismatch between workers and available jobs. Demand stimulus doesn't help workers need retraining to match the skills employers actually need.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (FRQ)

Assume the economy is in long-run equilibrium. An unexpected decrease in consumer confidence reduces aggregate demand. a) Using an AD-AS diagram, show the short-run effects of this decrease in aggregate demand on real GDP and the price level. b) If policymakers do nothing, explain the self-correcting mechanism that would eventually return the economy to long-run equilibrium. c) Explain ONE fiscal policy and ONE monetary policy the government could use to restore the economy to its original equilibrium more quickly.
View Model Answer
a) AD-AS Diagram Description: Draw a standard AD-AS diagram with LRAS vertical at potential GDP (Y*), SRAS upward sloping, and initial AD intersecting SRAS and LRAS at price level P1 and output Y*. A decrease in consumer confidence shifts AD leftward to AD2. The new short-run equilibrium shows: lower real GDP (Y2 < Y*) and lower price level (P2 < P1). This represents a recessionary gap output below potential, indicating unemployment above the natural rate. b) Self-correcting mechanism: With output below potential, there is a surplus of labor (unemployment above natural rate). Excess labor supply puts downward pressure on wages. As nominal wages fall, firms' production costs decrease, causing SRAS to shift rightward (downward). This process continues until SRAS has shifted sufficiently rightward that the economy returns to LRAS at potential GDP Y*, but at an even lower price level P3. In the long run, the economy self-corrects through wage and price flexibility, but this process may be slow and painful. c) Fiscal policy: Increase government spending (G) or cut taxes (T). Increased G directly adds to aggregate demand (AD = C + I + G + NX), shifting AD rightward back toward its original position. A tax cut increases disposable income, boosting consumption (C), also shifting AD right. The goal is to shift AD2 back to AD1, restoring Y* without waiting for the slow self-correction. Monetary policy: The Federal Reserve purchases Treasury securities in open market operations (expansionary OMO). This injects reserves into the banking system, increasing money supply. With more money available, interest rates fall. Lower interest rates reduce the cost of borrowing, stimulating investment (I) and interest-sensitive consumption, shifting AD rightward back toward the original equilibrium.

FRQ 2 (FRQ)

a) Explain the difference between the nominal exchange rate and the real exchange rate. b) If the U.S. dollar appreciates against the euro, explain the effect on U.S. exports and imports. c) Explain how a current account deficit is related to a country's capital account.
View Model Answer
a) The nominal exchange rate is the rate at which one currency can be exchanged for another e.g., 1 USD = 0.92 EUR. It is the market price of currency. The real exchange rate adjusts the nominal rate for differences in price levels between countries: Real Exchange Rate = Nominal Exchange Rate × (Domestic Price Level / Foreign Price Level). The real exchange rate determines the relative price of domestic goods in terms of foreign goods, making it more relevant for trade flows than the nominal rate. A real appreciation means domestic goods have become more expensive relative to foreign goods, regardless of nominal rate movements.

FRQ 3 (FRQ)

a) Explain what the money multiplier is and how it is calculated. b) If the Federal Reserve raises the reserve requirement from 10% to 20%, what happens to the money supply? Show with a numerical example. c) Explain ONE limitation of using the money multiplier to predict actual changes in the money supply.
View Model Answer
a) The money multiplier is the factor by which an initial deposit (or injection of reserves) is multiplied to produce the total increase in the money supply through the banking system. It is calculated as: Money Multiplier = 1 / Reserve Requirement. With a 10% reserve requirement, the money multiplier is 10 meaning each dollar of new reserves can support $10 of new deposits in the banking system through repeated lending and redepositing. b) Initial situation: Reserve requirement = 10%, Money multiplier = 1/0.10 = 10. If the Fed injects $1,000 in reserves, the potential increase in money supply = $1,000 × 10 = $10,000. After raising reserve requirement to 20%: Money multiplier = 1/0.20 = 5. The same $1,000 in reserves now supports only $1,000 × 5 = $5,000 in deposits a reduction of $5,000 in potential money supply. The higher reserve requirement contractionary effect: banks must hold more of each deposit as reserves, reducing the funds available for lending and thus the multiplication of deposits. c) Limitation: The money multiplier assumes banks lend out all excess reserves and that all money lent is redeposited in the banking system. In practice, neither assumption holds reliably. During recessions or financial crises (e.g., 2008-2009), banks hold excess reserves voluntarily (beyond the legal requirement) rather than lending them out, fearing loan defaults. Additionally, borrowers and depositors may hold cash rather than depositing funds. These 'leakages' mean the actual increase in money supply is typically less than the theoretical multiplier predicts, making the simple multiplier a poor predictor in uncertain economic conditions.

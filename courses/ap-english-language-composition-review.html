How to Get a 5 on This Exam

📊 What the Exam Tests Most

AP Lang tests rhetorical analysis, argumentation, and synthesis across three FRQ types: Synthesis (combine 6 sources into an argument), Rhetorical Analysis (analyze how an author uses rhetoric to achieve a purpose), and Argument (develop your own evidence-based argument). The MCQ section tests close reading of nonfiction prose identifying rhetorical devices, purpose, tone, and argument structure. Synthesis and Argument essays are about YOUR reasoning; Rhetorical Analysis is about the text's author's reasoning.

⚠️ Where Students Lose Points

Students in Rhetorical Analysis describe what the author says instead of analyzing how rhetorical choices create effects. 'The author uses statistics' earns nothing 'The author's use of mortality statistics shifts the audience's emotional frame from abstract policy to visceral human cost, making opposition feel morally untenable' earns points. For Synthesis, students quote sources without explaining how they connect to the argument.

🎯 What Separates a 3 from a 5

A 3 writer identifies rhetorical devices. A 5 writer explains the rhetorical effect of those devices in terms of the specific audience, purpose, and context. A 5 writer's Argument essay uses evidence that is both specific and clearly connected to the claim they never list examples without analysis. The 'so what?' of every piece of evidence is always explicit.

📅 Final 2 to 3 Weeks Strategy

Practice Rhetorical Analysis with 3 different essay types (political speech, scientific article, personal essay). For each, write a thesis that identifies the specific purpose and at least two rhetorical strategies the author uses to achieve it. Practice Synthesis by taking a position and only then reading the sources to find evidence. Time yourself: 40 min per FRQ, 60 min for MCQ.

Exam-Day Skills

  • Rhetorical Analysis thesis: Name the author, text type, purpose, audience, and at least two specific rhetorical strategies in one thesis sentence. Example: 'In her 2005 commencement address, Ursula Le Guin uses extended metaphor and deliberate syntactic disruption to challenge graduates to resist capitalist definitions of success.'
  • Effect-focused analysis: After identifying a device, always explain its rhetorical EFFECT: 'This [device] creates [effect], which [impacts the audience or advances the purpose] by [mechanism].'
  • Synthesis source use: Don't just summarize sources integrate them as evidence for your argument. Signal how each source supports your claim before quoting.
  • Argument essay structure: You need a clear thesis, body paragraphs that each advance the argument with specific evidence and analysis, and a conclusion that doesn't just restate but extends the argument's implications.
  • MCQ close reading: Read the question before the passage for efficiency. For 'the author's purpose' questions, consider the broader rhetorical situation who wrote this, for whom, and in what context before answering.

Essential Concepts

  • Rhetorical situation: speaker, occasion, audience, purpose, subject, tone
  • Argument structure: claim, evidence, warrant, backing, rebuttal
  • Modes of appeal: logos (logic), ethos (credibility), pathos (emotion)
  • Style and syntax: sentence variety, figurative language, diction choices
  • Source integration and citation in synthesis writing

Exam Day Tips

✓

For rhetorical analysis, analyze specific choices (not just 'the author uses pathos') name the device, quote it, explain its effect.

✓

Synthesis essays require citing at least 3 of the 6 to 7 sources don't summarize sources, use them as evidence.

✓

Argument essays: take a clear, debatable stance in the first paragraph; don't hedge.

✓

Time management: spend ~5 min planning each essay and ~5 min reviewing.

📋 How FRQ Scoring Works

Section I (45%): 45 MCQs in 60 min (reading passages then answering questions about rhetoric, argument, and style). Section II (55%): 3 FRQs in 120 min (~40 min each).

FRQ 1: Synthesis Essay (6 pts): Develop an argument using at least 3 of 6 to 7 provided sources. Scored on: (1) Thesis: makes a specific, defensible claim (1 pt), (2) Evidence & Commentary: uses source evidence with explanation of how it supports the argument (4 pts on a scale), (3) Sophistication: demonstrates complex understanding of the rhetorical situation (1 pt).

FRQ 2: Rhetorical Analysis (6 pts): Analyze how an author's rhetorical choices create a specific effect. Scored on: Thesis naming purpose and at least one rhetorical strategy (1 pt), Evidence & Commentary analyzing the effect of rhetorical choices with textual support (4 pts on a scale), Sophistication (1 pt).

FRQ 3: Argument Essay (6 pts): Develop your own argument on a broad topic. Same rubric: Thesis (1), Evidence & Commentary (4), Sophistication (1).

Scoring scale for Evidence & Commentary: 0: no evidence or evidence without comment; 1: evidence with minimal comment; 2: evidence with some relevant explanation; 3: evidence with consistently relevant explanation; 4: evidence with thorough, specific explanation connecting to the thesis.

Practice Questions

Multiple Choice (20 Questions)

Q1. Rhetoric is best defined as:

  • A. The formal rules of grammar and punctuation
  • B. The art of using language effectively and persuasively for a specific audience and purpose
  • C. The study of literary genres and narrative structure
  • D. The analysis of poetic meter and rhyme schemes
Show Answer

Answer: B: Rhetoric: the art of effective communication choosing strategies of language, argument, and delivery appropriate to audience, purpose, and context to achieve a desired effect.

Q2. The rhetorical concept of 'ethos' refers to:

  • A. The logical arguments and evidence in a text
  • B. The speaker's credibility and character as perceived by the audience
  • C. The emotional appeals made to the audience
  • D. The structure and organization of an argument
Show Answer

Answer: B: Ethos (Greek: character): the appeal based on the speaker's/writer's credibility, expertise, trustworthiness, and moral character. Writers establish ethos through demonstrated knowledge, fair-mindedness, and appropriate tone.

Q3. A 'rhetorical situation' consists of:

  • A. Only the author and the text
  • B. The author, audience, purpose, context/occasion, and constraints that shape a piece of communication
  • C. The argument, evidence, and conclusion
  • D. The introduction, body, and conclusion of an essay
Show Answer

Answer: B: The rhetorical situation (Burke's pentad/Bitzer's framework): exigence (the problem prompting communication), audience, purpose, speaker/writer, and context all shape what can and should be said and how.

Q4. Anaphora is a rhetorical device that involves:

  • A. The repetition of a word or phrase at the END of successive clauses
  • B. The repetition of a word or phrase at the BEGINNING of successive clauses
  • C. The use of understatement to emphasize a point
  • D. Addressing an absent or imaginary person
Show Answer

Answer: B: Anaphora: repetition at the beginning of successive phrases or clauses. 'We shall fight on the beaches, we shall fight on the landing grounds, we shall fight in the fields' Churchill's repetition of 'we shall fight' creates rhythmic urgency and resolve.

Q5. A 'concession' in argument is:

  • A. An admission of defeat
  • B. The acknowledgment of a point made by the opposing side, typically followed by a refutation or qualification
  • C. A type of logical fallacy
  • D. An emotional appeal to the audience's values
Show Answer

Answer: B: Concession-refutation: acknowledging the validity or appeal of the opposing view (conceding a point) before arguing why your position is nonetheless correct. Strengthens ethos by demonstrating fairness and intellectual honesty.

Q6. The term 'synthesis' in AP Language refers to:

  • A. Summarizing a single source in your own words
  • B. Drawing on multiple sources to support, qualify, or complicate your own argument
  • C. Analyzing an author's rhetorical choices
  • D. Writing a personal narrative response
Show Answer

Answer: B: Synthesis: integrating evidence from multiple sources into a coherent argument not merely summarizing sources but using them as evidence for your own claim, with proper attribution.

Q7. Which of the following is an example of the logical fallacy 'ad hominem'?

  • A. Arguing that a policy is flawed because its costs exceed its benefits
  • B. Attacking the character or person of the opponent rather than their argument
  • C. Using statistics without providing their source
  • D. Making a claim based on anecdotal evidence
Show Answer

Answer: B: Ad hominem (Latin: to the man): attacking the person making the argument rather than the argument itself 'You can't trust his climate policy because he's a hypocrite who flies private jets.' This is a fallacy because the argument's validity is independent of the arguer's character.

Q8. Periodic sentences are characterized by:

  • A. Simple subject-verb-object structure
  • B. Withholding the main clause until the end, creating suspense and emphasis
  • C. Short, declarative statements in a series
  • D. Beginning with subordinate clauses that clarify the main action
Show Answer

Answer: B: Periodic (climactic) sentences delay the main clause until the end: 'Despite years of warnings, despite the mounting evidence, despite the pleas of scientists and activists nothing changed.' The delay creates suspense and gives the final clause emphatic weight.

Q9. The 'enthymeme' in classical rhetoric is:

  • A. A type of three-part syllogism with all premises stated
  • B. A rhetorical syllogism with an unstated (assumed) premise that the audience supplies
  • C. An extended metaphor used to explain a complex concept
  • D. A series of questions posed to the audience
Show Answer

Answer: B: Enthymeme: an abbreviated argument relying on shared assumptions between speaker and audience. 'She must be honest she's a doctor' omits the major premise ('doctors are honest') which the audience supplies. Powerful because the audience participates in constructing the argument.

Q10. 'Tone' in a piece of writing refers to:

  • A. The speed at which the text should be read
  • B. The author's attitude toward the subject and/or audience as conveyed through word choice and style
  • C. The underlying message or lesson of the text
  • D. The formal or informal register of vocabulary used
Show Answer

Answer: B: Tone: the writer's emotional attitude toward subject and audience ironic, earnest, sardonic, reverent, skeptical conveyed through diction, syntax, and figurative language choices. Distinguishing tone from mood (the reader's emotional response) is essential.

Q11. Logos refers to:

  • A. Appeals based on the writer's character
  • B. Appeals to reason through evidence, data, logic, and well-structured argument
  • C. Appeals to the audience's emotions and values
  • D. Appeals based on the authority of external sources
Show Answer

Answer: B: Logos (Greek: word/reason): rational appeals statistics, causal reasoning, analogies, deductive/inductive arguments. A logos-heavy argument provides data, expert testimony, and logical structure rather than relying on emotional appeals.

Q12. Which of the following best defines 'diction'?

  • A. Sentence structure and arrangement of clauses
  • B. The author's choice of specific words and the level of language formality
  • C. The pattern of stressed and unstressed syllables
  • D. The use of figurative language and imagery
Show Answer

Answer: B: Diction: word choice including connotation (emotional associations), denotation (literal meaning), formality level, and specificity. Diction directly shapes tone and meaning; 'slender' vs. 'thin' vs. 'emaciated' carry different connotations despite similar denotations.

Q13. A 'red herring' fallacy involves:

  • A. Overgeneralizing from limited examples
  • B. Introducing an irrelevant issue to distract from the actual argument
  • C. Assuming that because two things are correlated, one causes the other
  • D. Appealing to popularity to support a claim
Show Answer

Answer: B: Red herring: deliberately introducing an irrelevant point to divert attention from the actual issue being argued. Common in political debate when a speaker pivots to a different topic to avoid addressing a damaging question.

Q14. The purpose of a 'claim of policy' argument is to:

  • A. Argue that something is true or false
  • B. Argue that a specific action should or should not be taken
  • C. Argue that something is good or bad, right or wrong
  • D. Explain the causes of a historical event
Show Answer

Answer: B: Claims of policy argue for or against a course of action 'The federal minimum wage should be raised to $20/hour.' They require establishing both a problem (claim of fact/value) and proposing a solution (the policy).

Q15. Metonymy is a figure of speech in which:

  • A. A word is repeated for emphasis
  • B. A part is used to represent the whole (synecdoche)
  • C. One thing is referred to by the name of something closely associated with it
  • D. A contradiction creates a paradoxical truth
Show Answer

Answer: B: Metonymy: substituting the name of something closely associated for the thing itself 'The White House announced' (= the President/executive branch announced); 'The pen is mightier than the sword' (pen = writing/ideas; sword = military force).

Q16. In the AP Language exam's argument essay, the most important requirement is:

  • A. Using all three rhetorical appeals equally
  • B. Developing a defensible claim with evidence and commentary that explains how evidence supports the claim
  • C. Writing in a formal academic style without personal pronouns
  • D. Addressing counterarguments in every paragraph
Show Answer

Answer: B: The argument essay requires: a clear defensible claim (thesis), specific evidence from your reading/experience/observation, and crucially commentary that explains the logical connection between evidence and claim. Unsupported assertions or mere summary earn minimal credit.

Q17. 'Juxtaposition' as a rhetorical strategy involves:

  • A. Repeating key words throughout an essay
  • B. Placing contrasting ideas, images, or characters side by side to highlight differences
  • C. Using technical vocabulary to establish expertise
  • D. Appealing to the audience's sense of national identity
Show Answer

Answer: B: Juxtaposition: positioning contrasting elements side by side to emphasize their differences. Lincoln's 'Four score and seven years ago' (founding ideals) juxtaposed with the battlefield carnage forces the audience to reckon with the gap between American promise and reality.

Q18. Which of the following correctly defines 'syntax' in rhetorical analysis?

  • A. The dictionary meaning of words
  • B. The arrangement of words and phrases to create particular effects including sentence length, structure, and punctuation
  • C. The use of irony and understatement
  • D. The speaker's voice and personality
Show Answer

Answer: B: Syntax: how sentences are constructed length (short = emphatic; long = complex reasoning), structure (parallel, periodic, cumulative), and punctuation patterns. Varied syntax creates rhythm; parallel syntax reinforces logical relationships.

Q19. A 'straw man' fallacy occurs when:

  • A. You cite authority figures to support an argument
  • B. You misrepresent an opponent's position to make it easier to attack
  • C. You use a single example to generalize
  • D. You assume a premise that requires proof
Show Answer

Answer: B: Straw man: distorting or simplifying the opponent's actual position into an easier target, then defeating that distorted version. 'My opponent wants open borders and no immigration enforcement' (misrepresenting a moderate reform position) is a classic straw man.

Q20. The rhetorical strategy of 'parallelism' is most effective for:

  • A. Introducing counterarguments
  • B. Creating rhythm, emphasis, and clarity by expressing coordinate ideas in the same grammatical form
  • C. Providing statistical evidence
  • D. Establishing the writer's academic credentials
Show Answer

Answer: B: Parallel structure: expressing related ideas in the same grammatical form 'We came, we saw, we conquered'; 'government of the people, by the people, for the people.' Creates rhythm, aids comprehension, and signals logical equivalence between ideas.

Free Response Questions

FRQ 1 (Synthesis FRQ)

The following sources address the question of whether social media platforms should be legally required to moderate misinformation. Source 1: A study showing that misinformation spreads 6x faster than accurate information on social media platforms. Source 2: An op-ed by a First Amendment scholar arguing that government-mandated content moderation constitutes unconstitutional viewpoint discrimination. Source 3: A European Union policy report recommending algorithmic transparency requirements and third-party fact-checking partnerships. Source 4: A survey showing 64% of Americans believe social media companies should take steps to restrict false information. Source 5: A statement by a platform CEO arguing that content moderation at scale is technically impossible without significant false-positive rates. Source 6: A commentary arguing that misinformation disproportionately targets minority communities and marginalized groups. Write an essay that synthesizes at least THREE of the sources to develop a position on the appropriate role of government in regulating social media misinformation. Avoid merely summarizing sources use them as evidence for your own argument.
View Model Answer
THESIS: While the harms of social media misinformation are well-documented and disproportionately severe for vulnerable populations, government-mandated content removal creates unacceptable First Amendment risks; the more defensible approach requires algorithmic transparency and platform accountability without direct government editorial control over speech. BODY PARAGRAPH 1: The problem is real and severe: Source 1's finding that misinformation spreads 6x faster than accurate content demonstrates that existing market mechanisms have failed to correct the information environment the platform's own incentive structures (engagement-maximizing algorithms) systematically amplify falsehood. Source 6 compounds this by showing the harm is not equally distributed misinformation campaigns targeting minority communities constitute a civil rights problem, not merely a nuisance. These sources together establish that the status quo imposes genuine social costs that justify policy intervention beyond voluntary corporate action. BODY PARAGRAPH 2: But government removal mandates cross a constitutional line: Source 2's First Amendment analysis correctly identifies the core danger. When the government decides which claims are 'misinformation' and mandates their removal, it necessarily exercises viewpoint discrimination the government becomes the arbiter of contested empirical and political claims. The history of government censorship shows that misinformation labels are applied most aggressively to minority political views. Source 5 adds a practical constraint: platform-scale moderation produces significant false positives even without government direction; adding government mandates would worsen this. BODY PARAGRAPH 3: Source 3's EU model offers a middle path: require algorithmic transparency (what the platform amplifies and why), mandate third-party fact-checking partnerships, and impose liability for failure to act on verified false information reported through established processes without giving government direct removal authority. This preserves private platform editorial judgment while creating structural accountability. Source 4's public support majority is important political context but cannot override constitutional analysis. CONCLUSION: Effective misinformation policy must be structurally sophisticated addressing the algorithmic amplification problem (Source 1) and the equity problem (Source 6) through accountability mechanisms (Source 3) without creating a government speech police (Source 2).

FRQ 2 (Rhetorical Analysis FRQ)

Read the following passage carefully. Then write an essay analyzing the rhetorical choices the author makes to achieve their purpose. Consider the author's use of diction, syntax, tone, structure, and appeals. [Passage: Excerpt from Martin Luther King Jr.'s 'Letter from Birmingham Jail' (1963)] 'I must confess that over the past few years I have been gravely disappointed with the white moderate. I have almost reached the regrettable conclusion that the Negro's great stumbling block in his stride toward freedom is not the White Citizen's Counciler or the Ku Klux Klanner, but the white moderate, who is more devoted to 'order' than to justice; who prefers a negative peace which is the absence of tension to a positive peace which is the presence of justice; who constantly says: 'I agree with you in the goal you seek, but I cannot condone your methods of direct action...'' Analyze the rhetorical strategies King uses in this passage to advance his argument.
View Model Answer
THESIS: In this passage from 'Letter from Birmingham Jail,' King employs precise antithetical diction, a confessional yet assertive tone, and sustained parallel structure to reframe the white moderate as a greater impediment to justice than explicit racists, thereby compelling his audience white clergymen to examine their own complicity. DICTION ANALYSIS: King's word choices are calculated to disturb his audience's self-conception. His use of 'confess' and 'gravely disappointed' at the opening establishes a tone of reluctant truth-telling rather than accusation this is not angry rhetoric but sorrowful correction, positioning King as the reasonable party forced by events to state an uncomfortable truth. The distinction between 'Ku Klux Klanner' (explicit enemy) and 'white moderate' (apparent ally) is devastating: by naming the moderate as 'the Negro's great stumbling block,' King deploys the language of obstacle-rhetoric to recharacterize inaction as a form of opposition. The white moderate's devotion to 'order' is placed in explicit tension with 'justice' a higher value whose invocation carries moral, legal, and theological authority. PARALLEL STRUCTURE AND ANTITHESIS: The extended contrast between 'negative peace...absence of tension' and 'positive peace...presence of justice' uses antithesis to redefine the debate's terms entirely. The moderates defend peace; King redefines the two types of peace to show that what the moderates defend is the empty form rather than the substance. The parallel 'who is...; who prefers...; who constantly says' construction builds through anaphora, each clause adding to the indictment while maintaining the syntactic dignity of measured, sorrowful observation. TONE AND ETHOS: The confessional opening ('I must confess,' 'regrettable conclusion') performs reluctance King does not want to criticize allies, but truth compels him. This strengthens his ethos as fair-minded even while the substance of the argument is deeply confrontational. By quoting the moderate's own words ('I agree with you in the goal you seek'), King demonstrates intimate knowledge of the moderate position, then reveals its logical contradiction: agreeing with the goal while opposing the only available means is effectively opposing the goal.

FRQ 3 (Argument FRQ)

'The most important factor in a student's academic success is not intelligence or natural ability, but the quality of their educational environment and the support systems available to them.' Write a well-developed essay that argues your position on this claim. Use evidence from your reading, experience, or observation to support your argument.
View Model Answer
THESIS: While intelligence and effort are meaningful contributors to individual outcomes, the evidence from education research, sociology, and history is overwhelming: the quality of a student's educational environment and support systems is the primary determinant of academic success because these structural factors determine whether any underlying potential can be realized. PARAGRAPH 1: Evidence from educational research: The Coleman Report (1966), despite its era's limitations, established that socioeconomic background and school resources explained more variance in achievement than any individual characteristic. More recent studies confirm: access to experienced teachers, small class sizes (Tennessee STAR study: significant effects of reduced K-3 class size persisting through high school), rigorous curriculum, and stable school funding are more predictive of outcomes than initial test scores. Students who transfer from low-resource to high-resource schools show dramatic achievement gains demonstrating the environment's causal role, not mere correlation with underlying ability. PARAGRAPH 2: The role of support systems: The research of Carol Dweck on growth mindset shows that students' beliefs about their own ability are themselves shaped by their environments a teacher who communicates high expectations and provides scaffolded challenge produces students who develop persistence and capability, while low-expectation environments produce self-fulfilling prophecy. The Harlem Children's Zone demonstrates that intensive wraparound supports (health, nutrition, family counseling, extended learning time) produce college-going rates among historically underserved students comparable to affluent peers. Environment doesn't just help students it creates the conditions under which natural ability can emerge and develop. CONCESSION AND REFUTATION: Intelligence clearly plays some role students at the extreme ends of the cognitive distribution show outcomes somewhat independent of environment. And individual effort matters. But the argument is about the primary factor: the range of environments available to American students is so wide from crumbling schools with 60% teacher turnover to well-resourced schools with experienced teachers and mental health counselors that environmental variation swamps individual variation for the vast majority of students. The same 'intelligent' student in each environment will have radically different outcomes. CONCLUSION: The uncomfortable implication of this evidence is political: if environment is primary, then unequal educational environments are not neutral facts but active mechanisms producing unequal outcomes. Acknowledging the environment's primacy makes educational equity not a charitable goal but a precondition for any claim that outcomes reflect merit.

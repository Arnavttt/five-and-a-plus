Must-Know Concepts

1.1 What a wave actually is

A wave is a vibratory disturbance that travels through a medium or field. It transfers energy, not mass. The medium itself only vibrates in place: a leaf on the ocean bobs up and down, it doesn't ride the wave to shore.

Pulse = a single disturbance. Periodic wave = a regularly repeating disturbance. Both transmit energy.

1.2 Mechanical vs. Electromagnetic waves
MechanicalElectromagnetic (EM)
Need a medium (matter) to travel
Examples: sound, water, springs, earthquake waves
Carried by particle vibrations		 Do NOT need a medium: travel through vacuum
Examples: light, radio, microwave, X-rays, UV, IR, gamma
Carried by oscillating electric & magnetic fields

Trap: Sound cannot travel through space (no medium). Light can. This shows up on every test as a "which can travel through vacuum?" question.

1.3 Transverse vs. Longitudinal waves
TransverseLongitudinal
Particle motion is perpendicular to wave direction.
Has crests (max up) and troughs (max down).
Examples: all EM waves (light, radio…), waves on a rope, S-waves.		 Particle motion is parallel to wave direction.
Has compressions (squished) and rarefactions (spread).
Examples: sound, compression in a spring, P-waves.
Pattern shortcut for analogies: "Transverse : light :: longitudinal : sound." If you see this analogy on a test, that's almost always the answer.
1.4 Wave anatomy (vocabulary)
Amplitude (A) Maximum displacement from rest position. Measured from equilibrium line to crest (or to trough). NOT peak-to-peak.
Wavelength (λ) Distance between two consecutive points in phase: crest to crest, trough to trough, or compression to compression. Unit: meters.
Frequency (f) Number of complete cycles per second. Unit: Hertz (Hz) = 1/s.
Period (T) Time for one complete cycle. Unit: seconds. T = 1/f.
Speed (v) How fast the wave moves through the medium. v = fλ. Depends only on the medium.
Crest Highest point of a transverse wave.
Trough Lowest point of a transverse wave.
Compression Region of maximum density in a longitudinal wave.
Rarefaction Region of minimum density in a longitudinal wave.
In phase Same point in the cycle, same direction of motion (0° or 360° apart).
180° out of phase Opposite points in the cycle: one going up while the other goes down. ½ wavelength apart.
Node Point in a standing wave that never moves (zero amplitude).
Antinode Point in a standing wave with maximum amplitude.

Critical rule on amplitude: amplitude is the distance from the rest line to the peak: NOT from trough to crest. If you see a peak-to-peak distance labeled, amplitude is HALF that number.

1.5 Pulse behavior: what changes vs. what stays

As a pulse travels in a uniform medium: speed stays constant, amplitude decreases as energy spreads out.

When a pulse hits a boundary / changes mediums:

  • Speed may change
  • Wavelength may change
  • Amplitude may change
  • Frequency NEVER changes: frequency is set by the source
  • Part of the pulse reflects, part transmits, part is absorbed
1.6 Reflection (Law of Reflection)

When a wave bounces off a boundary, angle of incidence = angle of reflection, both measured from the normal (a line perpendicular to the surface at the point of contact). \( \theta_i = \theta_r \)

Two types:

  • Regular (specular) reflection: smooth surface (mirror). Parallel rays in → parallel rays out. This is how mirrors form images.
  • Diffuse reflection: rough/irregular surface. Parallel rays in → scattered rays in all directions. This is how you see everyday objects.
Convention trap: "Angle of reflection = 30°": always measured from the NORMAL, never from the mirror surface. If the diagram labels 60° between the ray and the mirror, the angle from the normal is 30° (since they're complementary, 90° − 60°).
1.7 Refraction (bending of light)

Refraction is the bending of a wave when it changes speed as it enters a new medium. Direction change is caused by speed change.

The two-rule pattern (memorize):

Going from…SpeedBendsAngle of refraction
Fast (low n) → Slow (high n)DecreasesTOWARD normalSmaller than incidence
Slow (high n) → Fast (low n)IncreasesAWAY from normalLarger than incidence

Special case: If the ray enters at 0° (along the normal), no bending happens but speed still changes.

1.8 Index of refraction (n)

Defined as: \( n = \dfrac{c}{v} \) where \(c\) = speed of light in vacuum (3.00 × 10⁸ m/s) and \(v\) = speed of light in the material.

Patterns:

  • n is always ≥ 1 (vacuum has n = 1 exactly)
  • Bigger n → slower light → more optically dense
  • n has no units (it's a ratio of two speeds)
  • Two materials with the same n → no refraction, ray goes straight through (angle of incidence = angle of refraction)

Common values to know (NYS reference table): Air ≈ 1.00, Water = 1.33, Alcohol/Ethyl = 1.36, Glycerol = 1.47, Corn oil = 1.47, Lucite = 1.50, Crown glass = 1.52, Flint glass = 1.66, Zircon = 1.92, Diamond = 2.42.

1.9 Snell's Law

\( n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2 \). This is the master equation for refraction. Angles are measured from the normal. Subscript 1 = incident side, 2 = refracted side.

Equivalent forms (since \(n = c/v\) and \(v = f\lambda\)):

\( \dfrac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = \dfrac{v_1}{v_2} = \dfrac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} \)

1.10 Critical angle & Total Internal Reflection (TIR)

Critical angle = the angle of incidence that produces an angle of refraction of exactly 90°. Formula: \( \sin\theta_c = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} \) where light goes from medium 1 → medium 2.

TIR requires TWO conditions:

  • Light traveling from denser → less dense (high n → low n)
  • Angle of incidence greater than the critical angle

If both happen: no light refracts out: 100% reflects back. Used in fiber optics, prisms in binoculars, periscopes.

1.11 Diffraction

Waves spread out when they pass through an opening or around an edge. The smaller the opening (closer to the wavelength), the MORE the diffraction.

Pattern: Bigger opening relative to wavelength = small diffraction; opening close to wavelength = large diffraction.

1.12 Interference & Superposition

When two waves meet, the displacements add algebraically (Principle of Superposition).

TypePhaseResult
ConstructiveIn phase (0°)Bigger amplitude: crest + crest = taller crest
Destructive180° out of phaseSmaller amplitude: crest + equal trough = zero (cancellation)

Standing waves = two waves of same frequency & amplitude moving in opposite directions interfere to make a stationary pattern of nodes (no motion, fully destructive) and antinodes (max motion, fully constructive).

1.14 Polarization

Light is normally unpolarized: its electric field vibrates in all directions perpendicular to travel. A polarizing filter (like polarized sunglasses) only lets through vibrations in one plane, blocking glare.

Pattern: Polarization is proof that light is a transverse wave (longitudinal waves like sound cannot be polarized).

1.15 Dispersion

White light passing through a prism splits into colors because different wavelengths refract by different amounts. Higher frequency (violet/blue) → bends MORE because it has a slightly higher index of refraction in glass. Lower frequency (red) → bends LEAST. This is what creates rainbows.

1.16 Doppler Effect (bonus: short answer favorite)

If source & observer move toward each other → observed frequency increases (blueshift for light, higher pitch for sound). If moving apart → observed frequency decreases (redshift, lower pitch). The actual frequency emitted by the source never changes.

Formula Sheet With Meaning

Wave speed equation

\[ v = f\lambda \]

v = wave speed (m/s), f = frequency (Hz), λ = wavelength (m)

Use whenever you have any two of speed/frequency/wavelength and need the third. The single most-tested equation.

Period ↔ Frequency

\[ T = \dfrac{1}{f}, \quad f = \dfrac{1}{T} \]

T = period (s), f = frequency (Hz)

Use whenever the question gives period and asks for frequency (or vice versa). Also: f = (number of waves) / (time in seconds).

Index of refraction

\[ n = \dfrac{c}{v} \quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad v = \dfrac{c}{n} \]

n = index of refraction (unitless), c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s, v = speed of light in the material (m/s)

Use to find the speed of light in any material given its n, or to find n given v. Rearrange depending on what's asked.

Snell's Law (refraction)

\[ n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2 \]

n₁, n₂ = indices of the two media, θ₁ = angle of incidence (from normal), θ₂ = angle of refraction (from normal)

Use any time a light ray crosses a boundary between two materials. Always measure angles from the normal. Subscript 1 = side the light starts on.

Snell's Law: speed and wavelength forms

\[ \dfrac{\sin\theta_1}{\sin\theta_2} = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} = \dfrac{v_1}{v_2} = \dfrac{\lambda_1}{\lambda_2} \]

All four ratios are equal: pick whichever pair the question gives you.

Use when the problem gives you speeds or wavelengths in the two media instead of indices.

Law of Reflection

\[ \theta_i = \theta_r \]

θᵢ = angle of incidence, θᵣ = angle of reflection (both from normal)

Use any time a ray bounces off a mirror or surface. The two angles are always equal.

Critical Angle

\[ \sin\theta_c = \dfrac{n_2}{n_1} \quad (n_1 > n_2) \]

θ_c = critical angle, n₁ = incident medium (the denser one), n₂ = the less dense medium

Use to find the cutoff angle above which Total Internal Reflection occurs. ONLY valid when going from denser → less dense.

Patterns to Recognize

Pattern 1: Frequency & wavelength trade-off. If wave speed is constant (same medium), then \(f \uparrow\) means \(\lambda \downarrow\), and \(f \downarrow\) means \(\lambda \uparrow\). They're inversely proportional. The product is fixed.
Pattern 2: Frequency NEVER changes when a wave changes mediums. Frequency is set by the source. What changes is speed AND wavelength (both either grow or shrink together). So \(\lambda\) scales with \(v\): faster medium → longer wavelength.
Pattern 3: Speed-medium link. Light slows down in denser materials. Denser = higher n = slower. (Sound is the OPPOSITE: sound is faster in denser materials. Don't mix these up.)
Pattern 4: Refraction direction. Slower side → bends TOWARD normal. Faster side → bends AWAY from normal. The angle on the slower side is always the smaller angle.
Pattern 5: "Same n on both sides → straight line." If the question shows i = r and asks what the second medium could be, look for the one with the SAME index of refraction as the first. (Light Quiz Q5: corn oil n=1.47, glycerol n=1.47 → answer is glycerol.)
Pattern 6: Constructive vs destructive interference test. Two waves' displacements ADD. If both are positive (crest meets crest) → tall positive. If equal & opposite (crest meets equal trough) → zero. The math is just arithmetic at each point.
Pattern 7: Phase relationships. 0° apart = in phase. 90° = quarter wavelength apart. 180° = half wavelength apart (always opposite direction of motion). 360° = full wavelength = in phase again.
Pattern 8: Nodes and antinodes. Node = zero displacement, always destructive. Antinode = max displacement, alternates between fully constructive both ways. In a standing wave diagram, nodes are the "pinched" spots, antinodes are the bellies.
Pattern 9: Pulses transfer energy only. Whenever you see "a wave/pulse traveled… it transferred ___?" the answer is ENERGY ONLY. Mass never moves with a wave.
Pattern 10: Particle direction in a transverse wave. For a wave moving RIGHT: look at the wave shape just to the LEFT of the particle. That's where the particle is heading next. If left of the particle the wave is higher → particle moves UP. If left of the particle the wave is lower → particle moves DOWN. (This is exactly the rule you missed on Waves Q8.)
Pattern 11: Mechanical needs medium; EM doesn't. "Can it travel through space/vacuum?" → only EM (light, radio, etc.) can. Sound, water waves, springs all need matter.
Pattern 12: Polarization = transverse only. If a question mentions sunglasses, glare, or polarizing filters → answer is polarization. Polarization is ONLY possible for transverse waves (proof light is transverse).
Pattern 13: Diffraction increases as opening shrinks. Bigger opening → less spread. Opening size near wavelength → biggest spread.
Pattern 14: Dispersion = wavelength-dependent refraction. White light splits into colors because each color has a different index of refraction in glass. Violet bends most, red bends least.
Pattern 15: Critical angle only when going dense → less dense. If a question gives you a "find critical angle" prompt with light going from a low-n medium into a high-n medium, that's a trick: no critical angle exists in that direction.

Your Quiz Mistakes: Fix These First

Waves Quiz #1: Q8: Particle direction in a transverse wave

Your answer: B (up)    Correct: D (down)

The setup: A transverse wave was traveling to the RIGHT. Particle A was shown sitting in the lower half of the wave (on the downslope side / heading toward a trough). A dashed line showed where the wave shape would be a moment later.

Why D (down) is correct: For a wave moving right, the wave shape shifts to the right over time: so the future position of any particle takes on the value the wave currently has just to the LEFT of that particle. Just to the left of A, the wave is even lower (deeper in the trough). Therefore A is about to move DOWN, not up.

The bulletproof rule: "Wave moves right → look LEFT to predict particle motion." (Wave moves left → look RIGHT.) Whatever the wave looks like on the side the wave is coming FROM is where the particle is heading next.

Mental model: Imagine the wave shape sliding to the right like a strip of film. The dot stays at one x-position, but the strip slides past it. The next height it'll have is whatever was just to its left.
Light Quiz: Q3: Color of 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m light

Your answer: D (violet)    Correct: A (green)

The math: 5.0 × 10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm. The visible spectrum runs roughly 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red). 500 nm sits squarely in the GREEN region (~490 to 570 nm).

Why violet is wrong: Violet is at the SHORT-wavelength end (~400 nm = 4.0 × 10⁻⁷ m). You confused "small number in scientific notation" with "violet."

Memorize these three anchor points:

  • 4 × 10⁻⁷ m = 400 nm = violet
  • 5 × 10⁻⁷ m = 500 nm = green
  • 7 × 10⁻⁷ m = 700 nm = red

Anything in between, slot it in: 600 nm ≈ orange/yellow, 450 nm ≈ blue.

Light Quiz: Q7: Light from alcohol into flint glass

Your answer: C (speed increases, bends toward normal)    Correct: D (speed decreases, bends toward normal)

The setup: Light goes from alcohol (n = 1.36) into flint glass (n = 1.66).

Two-step decision tree (use this every time):

  1. Compare the n values. Going to a HIGHER n means going to a denser/slower medium. (1.66 > 1.36 → light slows DOWN.)
  2. Apply the bending rule. Slower → bends TOWARD normal.

So the correct combo is: speed DOWN + bends TOWARD normal. You got the direction right but the speed wrong: you swapped which side was slower.

Pattern lock: Speed direction and bending direction always pair the same way: "decreases & toward" together, OR "increases & away" together. You can never have "increases & toward" or "decreases & away." That immediately eliminates two of the four answer choices.
Waves Quiz #1: Q4 (close call): Reading amplitude from a diagram

The diagram labeled 0.20 m vertical and 1.5 m horizontal. The 0.20 m label was the distance from the equilibrium line to a crest → that IS amplitude = 0.20 m. The 1.5 m spanned 2.5 wavelengths → λ = 0.60 m. Correct answer: B.

Trap to avoid: Amplitude = (rest line) → (crest). NOT trough-to-crest (that would be 2 × amplitude). If a diagram shows the full peak-to-peak distance, divide by 2.

General quiz strategy fixes

1. Always look at the reference table values for index of refraction before guessing. The NYS table is provided; use it to look up exact n for water (1.33), alcohol (1.36), corn oil (1.47), glycerol (1.47), Lucite (1.50), crown glass (1.52), flint glass (1.66), zircon (1.92), diamond (2.42).

2. Always measure angles from the NORMAL. If the diagram labels the angle between the ray and the surface, that's the COMPLEMENT: subtract from 90° to get the angle from normal.

3. When two answer choices have the same direction-of-bending, look at the speed condition. Often the test gives all four combos (speed↑ + away, speed↑ + toward, speed↓ + away, speed↓ + toward). Only TWO are physically possible: "↑ + away" and "↓ + toward." Eliminate the impossible ones first.

Teacher-Style Practice (Scarpulla format)

Format mirrors your actual quizzes: short MCQs with a diagram reference, mixed conceptual + calculation, NYS-style answer choices. Try each before clicking the solution.

Practice Q1, diagram-style
A transverse wave is traveling to the LEFT. Point P sits on the upslope of a crest (rising part of the wave). In the next instant, point P will move:
  • A) left
  • B) right
  • C) up
  • D) down
Reveal answer

C: up. Wave moves LEFT, so look at the wave just to the RIGHT of P to predict its next position. To P's right the wave is higher (toward the crest), so P's next height is higher → P moves UP. The "look opposite to wave direction" rule is universal.

Practice Q2, v = fλ calculation
A periodic wave has a wavelength of 0.40 m and a frequency of 5.0 Hz. What is its speed?
  • A) 0.080 m/s
  • B) 2.0 m/s
  • C) 12.5 m/s
  • D) 20. m/s
Reveal answer

B: 2.0 m/s. v = fλ = (5.0 Hz)(0.40 m) = 2.0 m/s.

Practice Q3, Period from wave count
Six complete waves pass a fixed point in 3.0 seconds. What is the period of these waves?
  • A) 0.50 s
  • B) 2.0 s
  • C) 3.0 s
  • D) 18 s
Reveal answer

A: 0.50 s. Frequency = 6 waves / 3.0 s = 2.0 Hz. Period T = 1/f = 1/2.0 = 0.50 s. Easier shortcut: T = total time / # of waves = 3.0/6 = 0.50 s.

Practice Q4, Index of refraction
Light travels through a transparent material at 2.00 × 10⁸ m/s. What is the absolute index of refraction of this material?
  • A) 0.667
  • B) 1.00
  • C) 1.50
  • D) 6.00 × 10¹⁶
Reveal answer

C: 1.50. n = c/v = (3.00 × 10⁸)/(2.00 × 10⁸) = 1.50. (This would be Lucite or crown glass.)

Practice Q5, Snell's Law
A monochromatic light ray traveling in air strikes the surface of water (n = 1.33) at an angle of incidence of 30°. What is the angle of refraction?
  • A) 22°
  • B) 30°
  • C) 40°
  • D) 48°
Reveal answer

A: 22°. n₁ sin θ₁ = n₂ sin θ₂. (1.00)sin(30°) = (1.33)sin(θ₂). sin(θ₂) = 0.500/1.33 = 0.376. θ₂ = arcsin(0.376) ≈ 22°. Sanity: going to denser medium → angle gets smaller (bends toward normal). ✓

Practice Q6, Color from wavelength
Light has a wavelength in air of 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ m. This light is most likely:
  • A) violet
  • B) green
  • C) red/orange
  • D) infrared
Reveal answer

C: red/orange. 6.5 × 10⁻⁷ m = 650 nm. Visible: 400 nm violet → 700 nm red. 650 nm is in the red-orange range.

Practice Q7, Speed and direction at boundary
A light ray passes from crown glass (n = 1.52) into water (n = 1.33). What happens to the ray?
  • A) Speed decreases and ray bends toward the normal
  • B) Speed decreases and ray bends away from the normal
  • C) Speed increases and ray bends toward the normal
  • D) Speed increases and ray bends away from the normal
Reveal answer

D: speed increases & bends AWAY from normal. Going to a lower n (1.33 < 1.52) means less dense → light SPEEDS UP. And faster medium → bends AWAY from normal. Remember the pair: "↑ speed + away" always go together; "↓ speed + toward" always go together.

Practice Q8, Critical angle
What is the critical angle for light traveling from diamond (n = 2.42) into air?
  • A) 24°
  • B) 42°
  • C) 49°
  • D) 66°
Reveal answer

A: 24°. sin θ_c = n₂/n₁ = 1.00/2.42 = 0.413. θ_c = arcsin(0.413) ≈ 24.4°. Diamond has the smallest critical angle of common materials, which is why diamonds sparkle: most light gets TIR'd inside.

Practice Q9, Reflection convention
A light ray strikes a plane mirror, making an angle of 25° with the mirror's surface. What is the angle of reflection?
  • A) 25°
  • B) 50°
  • C) 65°
  • D) 90°
Reveal answer

C: 65°. Angle with the surface = 25°, so the angle from the NORMAL is 90° − 25° = 65°. By law of reflection θᵢ = θᵣ, the angle of reflection (from normal) is also 65°. This convention trap is exactly why Light Q9 is worth double-checking.

Practice Q10, Phenomenon identification
Parallel light rays strike a rough, uneven surface and scatter in many different directions. This phenomenon is best described as:
  • A) refraction
  • B) regular reflection
  • C) diffuse reflection
  • D) diffraction
Reveal answer

C: diffuse reflection. Rough surface still obeys θᵢ = θᵣ at each tiny point, but the normals at each point are tilted differently → reflected rays scatter. Refraction would require crossing into a new medium; diffraction requires an opening or edge.

Practice Q11, Standing wave node count
A standing wave between two walls shows 4 "loops" (antinodes) between the walls. How many nodes does the standing wave have (including the two endpoints at the walls)?
  • A) 3
  • B) 4
  • C) 5
  • D) 8
Reveal answer

C: 5. Each loop is bounded by two nodes; 4 loops require 5 nodes total. Walls are always nodes (fixed ends can't move).

Practice Q12, Wave type analogy
EM waves are to transverse waves as sound waves are to:
  • A) longitudinal waves
  • B) transverse waves
  • C) standing waves
  • D) mechanical waves only
Reveal answer

A: longitudinal waves. Light is the canonical transverse wave, sound is the canonical longitudinal wave.

Practice Q13, Interference
Two waves with equal amplitudes and frequencies meet 180° out of phase. The result is:
  • A) constructive interference; amplitude doubles
  • B) destructive interference; amplitude becomes zero
  • C) diffraction
  • D) resonance
Reveal answer

B: destructive interference, amplitude zero. Equal-and-opposite waves cancel completely. This is how noise-cancelling headphones work.

Practice Q14, Frequency of microwaves
A microwave has a wavelength of 1.5 × 10⁻² m in vacuum. What is its frequency?
  • A) 2.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz
  • B) 4.5 × 10⁶ Hz
  • C) 5.0 × 10⁻¹¹ Hz
  • D) 4.5 × 10¹⁰ Hz
Reveal answer

A: 2.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz. f = c/λ = (3.00 × 10⁸)/(1.5 × 10⁻²) = 2.0 × 10¹⁰ Hz.

Practice Q15, Vacuum traveler
Which of the following can travel through a vacuum?
  • A) sound waves
  • B) ocean waves
  • C) light waves
  • D) waves on a spring
Reveal answer

C: light waves. Only EM waves can travel through vacuum. The other three all require a medium (matter).

Short Answer Question Checklist

For every short answer, run through this list before moving on. If any box is unchecked, your answer is incomplete.

  1. Did I define the key physics concept the question is asking about (e.g., "refraction is the bending of light when…")?
  2. Did I identify the correct variables or the specific wave/light behavior (frequency, wavelength, n, angle…)?
  3. Did I explain the cause-and-effect relationship: what causes what, and why?
  4. Did I connect the answer back to the pattern in the question (diagram, value, scenario)?

Model short-answer responses
Model 1: "Why does light bend when it enters a new medium?"

Light bends because its speed changes when it enters a medium with a different optical density. This change in speed is what we call refraction. Specifically, when light passes from a less dense medium (lower n) to a denser medium (higher n), it slows down (\(v = c/n\)) and bends toward the normal. When light passes from denser to less dense, it speeds up and bends away from the normal. The direction of bending is governed by Snell's Law: \(n_1 \sin\theta_1 = n_2 \sin\theta_2\), where angles are measured from the normal.

✓ Defines refraction. ✓ Cause-and-effect (speed change → bending). ✓ Both directions explained. ✓ Formula included. ✓ Correct vocabulary. ✓ Answers the "why" directly.

Model 2: "Explain what happens to the wavelength of a wave when it slows down as it enters a new medium, assuming the frequency stays constant."

Frequency is set by the source, so it does not change when the wave enters a new medium: only speed and wavelength change. From the wave-speed equation, \(v = f\lambda\), if \(f\) is constant then \(\lambda\) must be directly proportional to \(v\). Therefore, when the wave slows down (\(v\) decreases), the wavelength decreases in the same proportion. Example: if light enters water and its speed drops to 75% of its vacuum speed, the wavelength in water also becomes 75% of the vacuum wavelength.

✓ States the key rule (frequency constant). ✓ Uses v = fλ formula. ✓ Explains directional pattern. ✓ Concrete example. ✓ Units implied correctly.

Model 3: "Explain the conditions required for Total Internal Reflection."

Total Internal Reflection (TIR) occurs when light traveling through a material is completely reflected at the boundary instead of refracting out. Two conditions must both be met: (1) the light must be traveling from a denser medium (higher index of refraction) into a less dense medium (lower n), and (2) the angle of incidence must be greater than the critical angle, defined by \(\sin\theta_c = n_2/n_1\). If both conditions are met, no light refracts into the second medium and 100% of the light reflects back into the first medium, obeying the Law of Reflection (\(\theta_i = \theta_r\)). TIR is the principle behind fiber optic cables, where light traveling down the fiber's core continually reflects off the cladding boundary instead of leaking out.

✓ Defines TIR. ✓ Lists BOTH required conditions explicitly. ✓ Formula for critical angle. ✓ Real-world application. ✓ Correct vocabulary throughout.

Model 4: "Describe the difference between constructive and destructive interference."

Interference is the result of two waves meeting in the same medium, and the displacements of the two waves add algebraically at each point (the Principle of Superposition). Constructive interference happens when the two waves are in phase (0° apart): crest meets crest, trough meets trough: and the resulting amplitude is the sum of the two individual amplitudes, producing a larger wave. Destructive interference happens when the two waves are 180° out of phase: crest meets trough: and the displacements cancel, producing a smaller wave (or zero amplitude if the two original waves had equal amplitudes). In a standing wave, the points of full destructive interference are called nodes; the points of full constructive interference are called antinodes.

✓ Defines interference and superposition. ✓ Contrasts both types. ✓ Specifies phase relationship for each. ✓ Connects to nodes/antinodes. ✓ Explains what happens to amplitude.

Model 5: "A wave travels from a fast medium to a slow medium. Describe what happens to its frequency, wavelength, and speed."

When a wave crosses from a faster medium into a slower one, its speed decreases (this is what causes refraction). Its frequency stays the same because frequency is set by the source and cannot change at a boundary. Using \(v = f\lambda\), if \(v\) decreases and \(f\) stays constant, then \(\lambda\) must also decrease proportionally. The wave's direction also changes: it bends toward the normal upon entering the slower medium.

✓ Addresses all three variables (v, f, λ). ✓ Uses the formula to justify the wavelength change. ✓ States the key rule about frequency. ✓ Adds direction-of-bending as a bonus.
